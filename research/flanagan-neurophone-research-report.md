## 0. Bottom line

1. **The core record is real and older than the legend.**
   - At 17, Flanagan was profiled in *Life* (14 Sep 1962, "Whiz Kid, Hands Down," pp. 69–72) [P8, P9].
   - He was granted US 3,393,279, filed March 1962 and issued July 1968 [P1].
   - By 1967 he was vice-president of research at Listening, Inc., Dwight Batteau's Navy-contract acoustics firm [P14]. He co-authored an Office of Naval Research report [P15].
2. **The patent is not what the modern product is.**
   - The 1962 patent describes an **RF electrical carrier (20–200 kHz) at "relatively high voltage," capacitively coupled through insulated electrodes on the skin** [P1].
   - Flanagan's own figures for his early devices are **1,500–2,000 V peak-to-peak**, with pads "on the temples next to the eyes" [P10].
   - Today's NF3 is a **9 V battery device driving ~40 kHz ultrasonic membrane transducers at 5–20 V** [P22, P23]. That is a different physical mechanism.
3. **The second "Neurophone patent" (US 3,647,970) is a speech-clipping patent.** It never mentions skin, nerves or body coupling [P2]. Calling it a Neurophone patent is Flanagan's and his friend G. Harry Stine's framing [S17], not the patent's.
4. **Lenhardt et al. (1991, *Science*) is real and relevant, but it is overclaimed.**
   - It showed **bone-conducted ultrasonic** speech perception, including in some profoundly deaf subjects [S26].
   - It does not mention Flanagan (abstract checked; full text not read).
   - It concerns mechanical vibration reaching the inner ear, not electrostatic nerve stimulation that "bypasses the ears." PhiSciences also misstates Lenhardt's institution [P22].
5. **Several prodigy claims fail or remain unsupported.**
   - **UNVERIFIED:** IQ 210, any Guinness record, the "$5,000 loan," and "1.5 million copies."
   - **UNVERIFIED (consistent context only):** *I've Got a Secret*.
   - **DISPUTED:** "the patent took twelve years" (it took about 6⅓). "Five years" under a secrecy order (filing to issue was 3½ years).
   - The invention date itself conflicts: 1958, 1959 or October 1961.
6. **New regulatory finding.** The FDA sent a **Warning Letter (30 Jan 2019)** to "Dr. Patrick Flanagan, Owner, Phi Sciences" over MegaHydrate and Crystal Energy disease claims and supplement manufacturing (CGMP) violations [P24]. This overturns the article's "no FTC or FDA action" line. No FDA record about the Neurophone itself was found [P25]. The 1960s–80s "FDA ban" is asserted only by Stine [S17, S18].
7. **"Prison planet": not found in any Flanagan text.** Do not attribute it to him (F35).
8. **DIY:** no credible Neurophone-specific build instructions exist.
   - The only widely copied "DIY Flanagan Neurophone" (Make:/Instructables, 2012) puts an 18 V ultrasonic PWM signal on skin through piezo "electrodes." It misdescribes the patent it cites [H1, H2]. **Do not build it.**
   - The credible, buildable neighbours are **mechanical**: bone-conduction transducers, surface exciters, and vibrotactile "tactile vocoders." These rest on a century of tactile-speech research [C1–C19].
9. **Safety:** the high-voltage electrode design is **not safely buildable at home in any form**. A battery-powered mechanical build is fine within the limits in Part 3.

## 1.4 Patent-office history

- **"Held up for twelve years": DISPUTED.**
  - Flanagan says the examiner "refused to issue the patent until 1967," relenting only after a working model was demonstrated. He says "the first patent took twelve years to obtain" [P10].
  - The record shows filing on 13 Mar 1962 and issue on 16 Jul 1968, **about 6 years 4 months** [P1, P5].
  - A long prosecution is real. Twelve years is not.
  - The story of a deaf Patent Office employee hearing it is **UNVERIFIED** (his account only).
- **Neurophone patent classified: UNVERIFIED.** I found no secrecy order for 3,393,279.
- **Secrecy order on the speech-waveform application: PARTIALLY.**
  - The Flanagan library hosts a facsimile [P7] of a USPTO **Secrecy Order** (35 U.S.C. 181–188) for Ser. **756,124**, applicant Gillis P. Flanagan, filed 29 Aug 1968. It is signed by First Assistant Commissioner Edwin L. Reynolds; the date stamp appears to read April 1969 but is partly illegible.
  - The same file has a **Notice of Allowability** (received 11 Jan 1971; examiner J. G. Baxter; claims 1–19 allowed). It states the application "will be withheld from issue during such period as the national interest requires."
  - The serial number, filing date, claim count and attorneys (Richards, Harris & Hubbard, Dallas) all match the issued patent [P2]. So the document is very probably genuine, though it came through the family's host rather than USPTO.
  - His 1996 account says the **Defense Intelligence Agency** imposed it and that he "was unable to work on the device or talk about it to anyone for another five years" [P10]. The form names **no agency**, and the patent issued 3½ years after filing. "DIA" is **UNVERIFIED**; "five years" is **DISPUTED**.
  - His promotional sheet calls these "Top Secret Orders" [P7]. The document is a standard secrecy order, not a "Top Secret" classification.

## 1.11 Independent science: what it does and does not show

1. **Electrical hearing predates Flanagan.**
   - Stevens (1937) and Jones, Stevens & Lurie (1940, "Three mechanisms of hearing by electrical stimulation") studied hearing produced by alternating current applied to the head [S29].
   - Puharich held patents on hearing by "electrical stimulation of the facial nerve system" (filed 1958 and Jan 1962). The examiner cited them against Flanagan's application [P1, S28].
   - Puharich & Lawrence published a clinical paper on "transdermal electrotherapy" for sensorineural hearing loss (*Acta Oto-Laryngologica*, 1969) [S28].
   - So "hearing" from electrical signals on the head is a genuine, documented phenomenon. The 1940 paper's title alone shows the field recognised several distinct mechanisms; I did not read the papers, so I make no claim about which one applies to the Neurophone.
2. **Lenhardt et al. 1991** [S26].
   - Abstract: "Bone-conducted ultrasonic hearing has been found capable of supporting frequency discrimination and speech detection in normal, older hearing-impaired, and profoundly deaf human subjects. When speech signals were modulated into the ultrasonic range, listening to words resulted in the clear perception of the speech stimuli and not a sense of high-frequency vibration."
   - *Science* published a critical comment by Dobie & Wiederhold and a Lenhardt reply (1992) [S27]. I did not read them.
   - Later Japanese work used imaging and masking to probe ultrasonic bone-conduction perception [S30] (titles only checked).
3. **How to relate this carefully.**
   - Lenhardt supports the claim that **amplitude-modulated ultrasound delivered mechanically through bone can be heard as speech, sometimes even by profoundly deaf people.** The mechanism involves the inner ear; the saccule is a hypothesis PhiSciences emphasises.
   - That is close to what the **NF3** does (ultrasonic membrane transducers on skin [P23]).
   - It is **not** what US 3,393,279 claims: an electrical RF carrier through insulated electrodes [P1].
   - It does **not** show that sound "bypasses the ears," that Flanagan's 1958 device worked as he said, or that the device raises IQ.
   - The PhiSciences line "Independently validated by Martin Lenhardt at the University of Virginia" is overclaimed (F38). Lenhardt was at the Medical College of Virginia / VCU [S26], and the abstract never mentions Flanagan or the Neurophone.
4. **Flanagan's own test claims.**
   - He describes a Tufts "beat frequency test" and says perception "is by means of skin and not by means of bone conduction" [P10].
   - No published report of that test was found. **UNVERIFIED.**

## 2.1 Three different physical classes

Keep these apart. The Neurophone literature constantly blurs them.

| Class | How energy reaches you | Examples | Home-buildable safely? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. ## 2.2 (a) Official or semi-official build information

- **The patent drawings are the only official schematic** [P1]. They show a vacuum-tube oscillator and modulator, back-to-back transformers, and insulated electrodes. They specify no voltage, which is itself a reason not to treat them as build instructions.
- US 3,647,970 [P2] contains a buildable **speech clipper** (filters plus clippers). It is harmless as audio processing, but it is not a body-coupling device.
- His **1996 history** describes the Brillo-pad and transformer prototype at ~1,500 V p-p [P10]. That is a hazard description, not instructions.
- **Stine, *Mind Machines You Can Build*** (1992/95; originally *Beyond the Frontiers of Science*) [H3]: the contents snippet shows radionic and psionic builds. The Neurophone is mentioned in passing, and no Neurophone chapter appears. **UNVERIFIED / unlikely.** I did not read the book in full.
- **Magazine reprints:** I found no *Radio-Electronics* or *Popular Electronics* construction article. *Electronics Now* classified ads (1996–97) sold "Neurophone" plans of unknown content [H7].

**Conclusion: no credible, official Neurophone build instructions exist.**

## 2.4 (c) Academic cutaneous-hearing and tactile-speech literature

All citations were checked in Crossref or PubMed. Abstracts were read where marked in SOURCES.

- **Gault (1920s)** [C1]: Northwestern psychologist Robert Gault's programme on understanding speech by touch, e.g. "Touch as a substitute for hearing in the interpretation and control of speech" (*Arch. Otolaryngol.* 1926), plus 1925 and 1927 papers. This is the true ancestor of "hearing through the skin." It is mechanical, not electrical.
- **Geldard, "Adventures in tactile literacy" (*American Psychologist*, 1957)** [C2]: proposed a learnable vibrotactile code on the body. I did not read the text, so I cannot quote it.
- **Tadoma** [C4]: deaf-blind users perceive speech by placing a hand on the talker's face and neck. Reed et al. (1982, 1992) quantified consonant and vowel identification by experienced users. It proves the skin can carry speech when the signal is rich enough.
- **Loomis (1974)** [C3]: tactile letter recognition under different presentation modes.
- **Bach-y-Rita et al. (*Nature*, 1969)** [C5] and **Bach-y-Rita & Kercel (2003)** [C6]: sensory substitution, tactile vision.
- **Kirman (1973)** [C7]: review of tactile speech communication.
- **Tactaid** [C8, C9]: commercial multichannel vibrotactile aids, compared by Weisenberger & Percy (1995) and Galvin et al. (1999). They supplied supra-segmental and some segmental speech information.
- **Tickle Talker** [C10–C12]: the University of Melbourne multichannel **electrotactile** speech processor (class A, low current, medically engineered). A dedicated safety study accompanied its development [C11], which is an example of what doing electrical skin stimulation properly involves.
- **Novich & Eagleman (2015)** [C13]: on the lower back, spatiotemporal vibration patterns transmit the most information. Motors needed **≥6 cm separation** to resolve two patterns reliably.
- **Perrotta, Asgeirsdottir & Eagleman (2021)** [C14]: deaf and hard-of-hearing participants using a Neosensory wrist device identified sounds at **up to 95%, average 70%** in a three-choice task, improving over a month.
- **Mechanoreceptor tuning** [C15–C18]: Verrillo (1963) and the four-channel model (Bolanowski et al. 1988; Gescheider et al. 2004).
  - The Pacinian (P) channel covers roughly the upper vibrotactile band, about 40 to several hundred Hz. Peak sensitivity is commonly given as ~200–300 Hz (the owner's "~250 Hz").
  - It shows spatial and temporal summation and declines with age.
  - Exact band edges were not re-read from the full papers, so treat the numbers as standard textbook values.
- **Bone conduction** [C19]: Stenfelt & Goode (2005) list five contributors to bone-conducted hearing (ear-canal radiation, ossicle inertia, cochlear-fluid inertia, cochlear-wall compression, cerebrospinal-fluid pressure) and judge cochlear-fluid inertia most important. **Bone conduction is hearing through bone into the cochlea, not a bypass of the ear.**

## 2.5 (d) Commercial and adjacent devices

No product URLs or prices are given, per the brief.

- **Bone-conduction headphones** (open-ear, transducer on cheekbone or mastoid). Class C, the same physics as [C19].
- **Neosensory wristband (Buzz)**: a class B vibrotactile sound-to-touch device, studied in [C14].
- **SubPac-style wearable tactile subwoofers** and **"bass shaker" tactile transducers**: class B, low frequency.
- **Surface exciters**: voice-coil drivers that vibrate whatever they are attached to. Useful DIY parts for both B and C.
- **NF3**: class C (ultrasonic) by its manual's description [P23]. Its efficacy claims are unsupported by published studies [S12].

**Closest real alternatives to a "Neurophone"**:
1. An audio-band bone-conduction setup, for "hearing without the ear canal."
2. A multi-motor tactile vocoder, for "speech through the skin."

Both are in Part 3.

# PART 3: Safety-first DIY lessons (low-voltage mechanical build only)

## 3.1 What this build is and is not

- **It is** a battery- or USB-powered audio amplifier driving **mechanical** transducers. It can show real vibrotactile perception and real bone-conduction hearing.
- **It is not** a Neurophone replica, and it **cannot test Flanagan's stronger claims**: electrostatic nerve hearing, bypassing the cochlea, IQ or learning effects.
- A positive result shows the same well-established physics as [C1–C19] and nothing more.

## 3.2 Components (typical specs; check each part's own datasheet)

| Part | Role | Typical specs / notes |
|---|---|---|
| Phone or MP3 player | Signal source; tone generator (any audio editor can make sine sweeps) | Use the headphone output and start at minimum volume. Run on its own battery during sessions. |
| **PAM8403 class-D module** [E7] | Amplifier, small transducers | **2.5–5.5 V supply** (USB 5 V). 3 W/channel into 4 Ω at 10% THD. Filterless, bridge-tied outputs: neither speaker wire is ground, so never tie a speaker "–" to ground or to the other channel. Four fresh alkaline cells exceed 6 V and can damage it. Diodes marks it "not recommended for new design," but modules remain common. |
| TPA3116D2 class-D module [E8] | Amplifier for larger tactile pucks only | 4.5–26 V; up to 2×50 W into 4 Ω at 21 V. **Far more power than skin needs.** Use only from a protected battery pack at the low end of its range, with a heatsink. Optional; most builds should skip it. |
| Surface exciter (voice-coil) | Vibrotactile on skin, or bone conduction on the mastoid | Impedance is printed on the part (commonly 4–8 Ω). Keep at or above the amp's rated minimum load. Contains a strong magnet. |

| ERM coin motors or LRAs | Multi-site tactile vocoder | ERMs run on low DC (commonly ~3 V) and change intensity with voltage. LRAs need an AC drive near their resonance, typically in the low hundreds of Hz (check the datasheet), ideally from a haptic-driver IC. |
| Microcontroller with ADC and PWM | Band-pass and envelope to motors | Any common hobby board. Drive motors through transistors or MOSFETs with flyback diodes, or a haptic driver; never directly from GPIO pins. |

| Inline attenuator or volume pot; straps; foam pads | Level control, mounting | Mount boards off the skin. |

## 3.3 Physics in brief

- **Skin is a low-pass, coarse-resolution channel.**
  - Mechanoreceptor channels together cover roughly 0.4–500+ Hz. The Pacinian channel is most sensitive around 200–300 Hz [C15–C17].
  - Speech energy that carries intelligibility runs from a few hundred Hz to several kHz, above what the skin resolves well.
  - That is why practical tactile aids (Tactaid; Neosensory) **split audio into frequency bands and map each band's envelope to a separate skin location** [C8, C13, C14]. This is the tactile vocoder.
- **Bone conduction is hearing.** Vibrate the skull and the cochlea responds, mainly through cochlear-fluid inertia [C19].
  - Plugging the ears usually makes bone-conducted sound *louder* (occlusion effect, related to Stenfelt & Goode's ear-canal pathway) [C19].
  - Masking noise in the ears reduces it. Both effects show the ear is doing the work.
- **Ultrasound via bone** can be heard as speech [S26], but see §3.6 for why that experiment stays out of the home build.

## 3.4 Step-by-step build

**Stage 1: single-channel vibrotactile and bone-conduction rig**
1. Power the PAM8403 module from a USB power bank: 5 V to VCC, ground to GND. Check the board's markings.
2. Connect the phone's headphone jack to the module's L/R inputs and ground, via a 3.5 mm breakout. Add an inline volume pot if the module lacks one.
3. Connect one exciter or bone-conduction transducer across **one channel's +/– outputs only**.
4. With the transducer **not touching you**, set the phone to minimum volume and play a 250 Hz sine. Raise the volume slowly until the transducer just buzzes.
5. Touch the transducer to a fingertip, then the forearm. Then strap it with light pressure to the mastoid bone behind one ear. Keep it **at low volume** (§3.6).
6. Run 10 minutes, then power off and **check the transducer and amp temperature by touch.** Anything more than warm means the power is too high.

**Stage 2: four-channel tactile vocoder (optional)**
1. Feed audio into the microcontroller's ADC through a simple bias network. Keep the level low.
2. In firmware, split the signal into about four bands (e.g. low, low-mid, high-mid, high speech bands) with IIR band-pass filters or a short FFT.
3. Rectify and low-pass each band to get an envelope.
4. Map each envelope to a PWM duty cycle for one motor, via a transistor or MOSFET driver with flyback diode, or a haptic driver.
5. Place motors **≥6 cm apart** on the forearm or upper back [C13], held in a fabric sleeve or strap.
6. Power everything from the USB power bank. Keep motor current within the board's and power bank's ratings.

## 3.5 Experiments

1. **Frequency sweep (sensitivity curve).** Play steady sines at 20, 40, 80, 160, 250, 400, 700 and 1,000 Hz at a fixed amplifier setting. For each, raise the volume until just detectable on the fingertip and log the setting. Repeat on the forearm and lower back.
   - Expect the most sensitivity in the low hundreds of Hz on glabrous skin, and less on hairy skin and the back [C15–C18].
2. **Site comparison.** Compare fingertip, palm, forearm, upper back and sternum (over clothing). Repeat the two-motor spacing test: at what separation can you tell two motors apart? Compare with Novich & Eagleman's ≥6 cm on the back [C13].
3. **Tactile versus bone conduction.** Play spoken words at the same setting with the transducer on the forearm, then on the mastoid.
   - Forearm: you should feel a buzz, but words should be unintelligible.
   - Mastoid: you should *hear* them.
   - Then insert foam earplugs. Bone-conducted speech should get louder (occlusion). This is the cleanest home demonstration that the ear is involved.
4. **Blinded word identification.**
   - A helper uses a script to pick words at random from a closed set (e.g. 4 or 8 words). The subject wears foam earplugs **and** earmuffs and cannot see the screen. Use passive attenuation only; never loud masking noise.
   - Run at least 40 trials per condition (forearm single-channel; forearm 4-motor vocoder; mastoid bone conduction). Score percent correct against chance (25% for 4 words) and compute a binomial p-value.
   - Repeat over several days to see training effects, as in [C14].
5. **What not to conclude.** Above-chance mastoid scores show bone-conduction hearing. Above-chance vocoder scores show tactile speech cues. Neither tests Flanagan's claims.

## 3.6 Safety

**Do NOT replicate the Neurophone patent's design.** No electrodes of any kind, and no high-voltage, RF or mains-derived source touching the skin. Reasons:
- **Flanagan's own designs ran at 1,500–2,000 V peak-to-peak** with pads "on the temples next to the eyes" [P10]. The patent asks for "relatively high voltage" and at least 1 W, and itself includes a resistor "to reduce any dangerous voltage spikes" [P1].
- **Insulation is not protection at RF.** At 20–200 kHz, a Mylar-insulated electrode is a capacitor, so current still flows into tissue (that is how the patent works). High-frequency current causes **heating and RF burns** rather than the familiar shock. Insulation can also fail or arc at kilovolt levels. See Fish & Geddes (2009) on how current reaches and travels through the body, skin breakdown, nerve stimulation and cardiac effects [E6].
- **Current path.** Electrodes on the arms or torso can route current across the chest. Cardiac arrhythmia is the main lethal risk of body current [E6].
- **Implants.** People with pacemakers, ICDs, cochlear implants, neurostimulators or other active implants should not use electrical skin stimulation at all. Even with this mechanical build, keep magnet-bearing transducers away from implanted devices. That is a general precaution, not separately sourced here; follow the implant maker's guidance.
- The 2012 **Make:/Instructables TL494 "Neurophone"** [H1] is also excluded. It is an 18 V PWM source on skin contacts, built without measurement or medical review.
- **Electrotactile aids** (e.g. the Tickle Talker) needed formal safety engineering [C11]. They are not a home project.

**Power.** USB power bank or protected battery only. No wall adapters, bench supplies or transformers connected while anything touches the body.

**Hearing.** Bone conduction reaches the cochlea [C19], so it can damage hearing like any headphone.
- WHO: up to **80 dB for 40 hours/week** is safe, but **90 dB only 4 hours/week**. Keep device volume at **no more than 60% of maximum** [E4].
- NIOSH occupational limit: **85 dBA averaged over 8 hours, with a 3-dB exchange rate** (each +3 dBA halves the allowed time) [E1, E2].
- You cannot easily measure bone-conducted level with a sound-level meter, so stay at the **lowest level that works**, in **short sessions** (≤15–20 min).
- **Stop immediately** at ringing, muffled hearing, ear fullness, dizziness or headache.

**Placement.** Not over the **eyes**, the **throat or carotid area** (front and sides of the neck), or **broken, irritated or numb skin**.
- The NF3 manual lists "neck" as a "safe position" and describes a "tip of the tongue test" at 20 V [P23]. Both conflict with these rules. Don't copy them.
- Use light strap pressure only.

**Heat.**
- Voice-coil transducers and class-D chips heat up under continuous drive. The TPA3116D2 has thermal shutdown [E8], which tells you it can get hot.
- Keep boards off the skin and ventilated. Check transducer temperature after each session, and stop if anything is more than warm.

**Ultrasound.** Lenhardt-style ultrasonic bone conduction [S26] is **not recommended at home**. There is no practical way to measure the delivered intensity, and the hearing-risk picture for intense ultrasound coupled to the skull is not established. Hobby piezo drivers can also irritate or heat skin (the Make: author warns of irritation [H1]).

**Not safely buildable at home:** any electrostatic or RF electrode Neurophone, the TL494 skin-contact circuit, electrotactile arrays, and high-intensity ultrasonic contact transducers. **Safely buildable within the above limits:** the audio-band bone-conduction rig and the vibrotactile vocoder.

## 3.7 Epistemic status

This build can demonstrate, reliably and cheaply:
- that skin carries coarse vibration information;
- that multi-site coding improves it;
- that bone conduction lets you hear speech with the ear canal blocked.

It can neither confirm nor refute:
- that Flanagan's RF electrode device produced hearing by direct nerve stimulation;
- that the Neurophone "bypasses" the cochlea;
- any learning, IQ or "whole-brain" claim.

No published controlled study of any Neurophone was found, and Flanagan said in 2014 that none existed [S12].
