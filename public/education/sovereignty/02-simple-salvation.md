# Simple salvation teachings of Jesus

## Short version

In the Gospels, Jesus presents a direct invitation: human beings fall short of moral perfection, the consequence is separation from God, and the remedy is trust in him rather than self-justification or elaborate systems. The offer is available without requiring institutional monopoly or secret knowledge.

Key statements commonly cited:

- "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life." (John 3:16)
- "I am the way, and the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me." (John 14:6)
- "The Son of Man came to seek and to save the lost." (Luke 19:10)
- The summary of the law as love of God and love of neighbor (Matthew 22:37–40).

Salvation is described as a gift received by faith, not earned by works. The practical outworking is repentance (a change of direction) and a life oriented toward that love.

A short supporting chain often used in simple presentations (sometimes called the "Romans Road") runs through the wider New Testament:

- "All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." (Romans 3:23)
- "The wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." (Romans 6:23)
- "God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us." (Romans 5:8)
- "If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved." (Romans 10:9)

This is the minimal baseline used in this track. It requires no specialized vocabulary beyond the text itself.

## Expanded notes

Different Christian traditions disagree on secondary questions — sacraments, church structure, the exact mechanism of atonement, the role of works after faith. Those disagreements are real and long-standing. The lesson above deliberately stays with the statements that appear across the major traditions as the core invitation.

### Primary texts to read directly (short list)

These are short enough to read in one sitting:

- **Gospel of John**, chapters 1–3 and 14–17 — the clearest sustained statements of identity, belief, and relationship to the Father.
- **Gospel of Luke**, chapter 15 (parables of the lost) and chapter 19:1–10 (Zacchaeus) — the "seek and save" pattern in narrative form.
- **Matthew 5–7** (Sermon on the Mount) — the ethical shape that follows the invitation.
- **Romans 3–6 and 10** — the densest short statement of the gift-versus-works framing in the wider New Testament.

Any standard English translation is fine. Public-domain options that can be read offline once downloaded include the King James Version (KJV), the American Standard Version (ASV), and the World English Bible (WEB). Many free online readers also host these texts; the important thing is the primary chapters themselves, not a particular website.

### Mainstream and dissenting readings

Mainstream Christian teaching across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant lines affirms the centrality of Christ's death and resurrection for reconciliation with God, and treats the statements above as the core of the invitation. Critical and non-Christian academic readings treat the same texts as ethical teaching, later theological construction, or myth. Both literatures are large. This lesson does not attempt to settle those debates; it only isolates the plain textual core so a reader can encounter it without first adopting a school.

If this baseline is useful as a personal orientation, keep it simple. If it is not, the rest of the track does not depend on it.

## Sources

**Primary**

- The four Gospels, especially John 1–3, 14–17; Luke 15 and 19; Matthew 5–7 and 22:37–40.
- Romans 3–6, 10; also Ephesians 2:8–9 and Galatians 2–3 for parallel statements on faith and works.

**Translations**

- Any standard modern or traditional English translation. Public-domain choices: KJV, ASV, World English Bible (WEB).

**How to use the sources**

Read the short Gospel chapters first. Only after that, if desired, look at confessional summaries or critical commentaries. The habit is the same as the rest of this track: primary text, then interpretation.
