# Robert Allan Monroe (1915–1995): Broadcaster and Consciousness Entrepreneur

*Research report for Juan (KOOLSKULL / 2kool productions / K-OS-III). 2026-10-06 PT.*
*Editorial frame: K-OS-III `docs/EPISTEMIC_STANCE.md` [40]. The mainstream account and the owner's dissenting synthesis appear side by side, each with attribution. The owner's view is labeled as interpretation, not consensus.*

**How to read the sources.** *Primary* means Monroe's books and patents, period trade press, declassified Army/CIA files, and newspaper clippings. Many clippings survive only through Monroe Institute (TMI) archive uploads, which TMI selected and dated. *Secondary* mainly means Ronald Russell's *The Journey of Robert Monroe* (2007) [1], which draws on Monroe's taped reminiscences and is closely tied to TMI, an institution with a stake in his reputation. The biographical sheet in the CIA file [3] reads as a résumé Monroe or TMI supplied, not as government findings. Anything sourced only to Monroe, TMI, or Russell is treated as **self-reported** until independently confirmed.

## (a) Early life

**Birthplace.** Robert Allan Monroe was born on **30 October 1915 in Wabash, Indiana**. Three sources agree. A 1984 Army INSCOM information paper states it outright [2]. A Who's Who–style résumé preserved in TMI's archive says "b. Wabash, Ind., Oct. 30, 1915" [6]. Russell explains that all five children were "born in Georgia's family home in Wabash, where she returned to live in the final stages of all her pregnancies" [1, pp. 37–38]. The Psi Encyclopedia says he was born in Lexington, Kentucky [7], and so does a Lucid Mind Center biography reproduced in TMI's newspaper archive [25]. Lexington was where the family *lived*: his father taught Romance languages at Transylvania College [1]. Since the Psi Encyclopedia cites Russell as its source, its Lexington claim contradicts the very book it relies on. TMI itself says only that he "spent his childhood in Kentucky and Indiana" [9].

**Family.** His father, Robert Emmett Monroe, was a language professor. His mother, Georgia Helen Jordan, qualified in medicine in 1906 and played cello; Russell says her husband let her practice only during WWI [1]. The brief's "third of four children" needs correcting. Russell lists five births: Dorothy (1909), Margaret (1911), Robert (1915), Georgia Helen (1919, died before age two), and Emmett Paul (1923). Bob was therefore third of five, or third of four who survived infancy [1].

**Youth and college.** At Ohio State he moved from engineering to journalism [3]. Russell says he flunked out while hospitalized with facial burns, spent about a year as a hobo, then returned and finished in English while working on *The Lantern*, campus radio, and student theater [1]. His degree is a 1937 B.A. [6][9]; a 1943 clipping saying 1935 [12] is an outlier. Nothing I found supports the "pre-med" claim that circulates online.

## (b) Broadcasting: the steelman, item by item

The strongest honest version of the owner's position: Monroe went from Ohio station writer to network serial author, packager-producer, national network programming chief, and station and cable owner. Each claim below carries a verification status.

**Early jobs (1937–39). SELF-REPORTED.** Russell places him at WHK Cleveland and WLW Cincinnati [1]; the résumé says "Writer and dir. of radio programs, 1937-40" [6].

**Rocky Gordon (NBC, c. 1940–43). VERIFIED (authorship).** The cover feature of *Radio-Craft* for November 1940 shows "Robert Monroe, author of N.B.C.'s dramatic serial 'Rocky Gordon,'" recording locomotives at Weehawken for sound effects [11]. A 1943 clipping lists the show "at 5 p.m., Monday through Friday over NBC" [12]. The claim that it preceded the "LOWELL THOMAS – AMOS N' ANDY program block" [3] is *unverified*.

**Scramble (NBC Blue, c. 1942–43). CONFLICTING.** *Broadcasting* (7 Sept 1942) confirms that the show existed: "BLUE's other air-minded show, Scramble, dramatizes the war adventures of Army Air Forces pilots" [13]. Two 1943 clippings in TMI's archive call Monroe its "originator, writer, director, and even actor," with Robert Ripley as host, and say "Monroe conceived the program" for the National Aeronautic Association's Junior Air Reserve [12]. Neal Thompson's Ripley biography instead gives the creation to Ripley's manager Doug Storer and to Ripley [14]. Both accounts may be partly right, but the sources disagree.

**Donahue & Coe; Robert Monroe Productions; RAM Enterprises. DATES CONFLICT.** The résumé gives: production manager at Donahue & Coe 1940–43, Robert Monroe Productions 1946–49, RAM Enterprises 1950–54 [6]. Russell puts RAM in 1943 and Monroe Productions in 1944 [1]. Wikipedia gives RAM as 1953 [8].

**High Adventure (Mutual 1947–; NBC 1950; Mutual 1953–54). VERIFIED.** On 17 January 1950, *Radio Daily* reported the Shulton (Old Spice) sponsorship on NBC starting January 29: "Program will be written, directed, produced and musically scored by Robert Monroe" [15]. Jerry Haendiges's log supplies the run dates and George Sanders as narrator from 1953 [18].

**Nightmare (Peter Lorre, Mutual). PARTIALLY VERIFIED.** *Radio Daily* (23 Feb 1952): "Bob Monroe mulling over idea of filming Peter Lorre's 'Nightmare' series now on Mutual radio" [16]. That ties him to the show, though it is not a formal producer credit.

**John Steele, Adventurer (Mutual, 1949–). VERIFIED (secondary log).** Haendiges lists "Producer: Robert Monroe" and "Music: Sylvan Levin" [17]. The brief did not mention this show. Note that someone else is credited with the music here.

**Take a Number / Meet Your Match / MGM Screen Test / Starlight Theatre (Madeleine Carroll). SELF-REPORTED.** All four appear on Monroe's own list [3], and Russell adds that Take a Number offered prizes worth up to $60,000 [1]. I found no independent producer credit for any of them.

**Volume: "400 programs," "28 shows weekly." UNRESOLVED / SELF-REPORTED.** The CIA-file biographical sheet reads, exactly: *"He went to New York in 1939, where he was the creator and producer of some 400 radio and TV network programs in the ensuing twenty year period. In addition to directing and writing, he composed all of the orchestral music for his programs, much of which is still in use in television and motion pictures."* [3]. The same sheet says Robert Monroe Productions "at its peak operation, was producing as many as 28 network shows weekly" [3]. The peak is disputed. Russell gives both "twenty-eight shows broadcast every week" and "a record number of nineteen programs weekly" [1, pp. 74, 77]; TMI's older biography says 19 weekly [10]; Wikipedia says 28 *monthly* [8]. Whether "programs" means series or episodes is never specified.

**Music credits. ONE PERIOD CREDIT FOUND.** The one concrete contemporary credit is *Radio Daily*'s "musically scored by Robert Monroe" for the 1950 NBC run of *High Adventure* [15]. Russell tells of Monroe composing emotional "music sequences" for a library of musical phrasings. In his account the American Federation of Musicians blocked the library, so Monroe recorded it in Cuba while a nephew of Fulgencio Batista looked on [1, p. 74]. That story is *unverified*. I found no ASCAP or BMI entries via web search, and I did not query those databases directly. The Copyright Office records I found are all 1980s Hemi-Sync/Metamusic works registered to Interstate Industries as "employer for hire" [39]. The claim that his music is "still in use in television and motion pictures" [3] is *unverified*.

**Name That Tune. CONTRADICTED.** Russell credits Monroe with "creating Take a Number, Meet Your Match, and Name That Tune" [1, pp. 73–74], and the Psi Encyclopedia repeats it [7]. Every independent source points elsewhere. Wikipedia names Harry Salter (with Roberta Semple Salter) as creator and executive producer for 1952–59, with an NBC Radio premiere on 20 December 1952 [22]. The NYPL calls Salter "the original producer and conductor for Name That Tune" [23]. The University of Wyoming's American Heritage Center calls him its "creator" and holds 196 boxes of his papers, including 1952–58 scripts and 1953 game instructions [24]. Name That Tune is also missing from Monroe's own self-supplied list [3], from TMI's older biography [10], and from the résumé [6]. This should be read as an error that entered the authorized biography. **K-OS-III should not repeat it.**

**Mutual vice president. VERIFIED (c. 1955–56).** The 1956 *Broadcasting Yearbook* lists "Robert Monroe, vp. in charge of prog." at Mutual and "Robert Monroe, pd" at WOR [21]. The 1955 Yearbook has Herbert Rice in that job [20]. The résumé gives "v.p. charge programming activities, dir. MBS, Inc. 1954-56" [6]. A clipping announcing his appointment is dated "1952" in TMI's archive [12], which conflicts with the other sources. He does not appear on the 1956 board-of-directors list [21], so the "Director" title rests on his own claims.

**Stations and cable. LARGELY VERIFIED.** The résumé lists Carolina Radio (1958–62) and Southern States Radio Corp. (1961–75) [6]. A 1961 *Richmond News Leader* item reports a Monroe-headed corporation buying WEZL (renamed WRGM) and calls him "also owner of a Durham, N. C., station"; a 1963 FCC notice lists him as Southern States Radio's president [12]. Jefferson Cable clippings show a Charlottesville system planned in 1962 (seven channels plus FM) and completed in early 1963, Waynesboro in 1964, 90% sold to a finance company in 1965 with Monroe staying on as president, and a sale in 1975 [26]. So the obituary's "established cable television … in 1967" [25] is contradicted. Russell's "second twelve-channel cable TV system" in the U.S. [1] is *unverified*.

## (c) Out-of-body experiences, the Institute, Hemi-Sync, and government interest

**The books.** In *Journeys Out of the Body* (Doubleday, 1971) [27], Monroe describes his early experiences, including: *"I smoothly floated up over the bed, and when I willed myself to stop, I did, floating in mid-air. It was not a bad feeling at all, but I was nervous about falling suddenly."* [27]. He openly considered that he might be ill: "If it wasn't real—just a hallucination or dream—I was in trouble." [27]. *Kirkus* (1 Oct 1971) was dismissive: "Mr. Monroe is a radio writer-director but there's a lot of scientific garblese coming over these frequencies" [29]. The often-quoted "blew my cover" line comes from **Monroe's own Foreword to a later edition**, not from Tart's introduction: "The publication of the book quite thoroughly 'blew my cover' as a reasonably orthodox business executive" [27].

**Laboratory tests.** Charles Tart's introduction [27] describes eight University of Virginia EEG sessions (1965–66). Monroe had no OBE for seven nights, then two brief ones; he "did not report on the target number" (five digits, on a shelf) but correctly said the technician was in the corridor with a man, later identified as her husband. Tart: "As a parapsychologist, I cannot say that this 'proves'" anything. A 1968 session also failed to read the target [27]; the Psi Encyclopedia's summary of Tart's 1998 account agrees [7].

**The Institute.** It began as Whistlefield Research Laboratories before becoming the Monroe Institute of Applied Sciences [28]. Founding dates conflict: "became active in 1972" (later Foreword) [27], 1973 (CIA-file sheet) [3], 1974 (older TMI bio) [10], 1971 (WIRED) [37].

**Patents.** US 3,884,218 (filed 1970, granted 1975) amplitude-modulates a pleasant sound with EEG sleep waveforms; it does not use binaural beats [30]. US 5,213,562 (1993) credits H. W. Dove with discovering binaural beats in 1839 and says Monroe "coined the term frequency following response, or FFR" [31]. US 5,356,368 (1994) states: "The inventor has coined the term HEMI-SYNC" [32].

**Government interest. VERIFIED.** A 26 August 1983 Army executive summary says activity with Monroe's techniques "began in April 1978," that he "was granted a SECRET security clearance" on 11 September 1980, and that he was paid "$24,400.00" for training [4]. A 1984 paper adds the clearance was "based on a NAC completed on 18 January 1980" [2]. Lt. Col. Wayne McDonnell's 1983 assessment concluded there was "a sound, rational basis in terms of physical science parameters for considering Gateway to be plausible" [5]. The same document reports that, according to a TMI trainer, subjects asked to read ten numbers while out of body "none have ever succeeded in getting all ten correct" [5]. The assessment is an internal opinion by one officer, not an independent scientific validation.

## (d) The owner's framing *(labeled: Juan's interpretation, not historical consensus)*

Juan's thesis: Monroe deserves remembrance as a pioneer of American broadcast entertainment, in the company of "greats" such as Walt Disney, not merely as "the OBE guy"; a working artist of golden-age radio and early TV, and personally humble. The best evidence for that reading:

- **Craft across roles.** He wrote, directed, produced, and scored network drama. The 1950 *Radio Daily* credit is period proof of all four on one show [15]. He also recorded field sound for authenticity [11].
- **Early adopter.** Russell has him among the first to use magnetic tape in radio, splicing rehearsal takes [1]; he later entered cable TV in its infancy [26].
- **One career, two halves.** He composed emotionally targeted music cues in the 1950s, and Hemi-Sync/Metamusic came later. Russell draws that line himself, calling the radio cues "a foretaste of his later efforts in the early development of Metamusic" [1, p. 74]. The owner can reasonably present Monroe as a sound designer throughout.
- **Humility, in his own words and others'.** On naming the Institute: "Using the name Monroe was not an ego factor, but simply the quickest way to clear the title officially" [28]. Late in life: "I discount very highly the appellation of guru… I surely shudder at that. I try to demystify things" [25]. On himself: "I was a conventional person, convinced of conventional reality" [1]. He joked about his board's likely reaction: "Who would want such an unstable person running their multi-million dollar operation!" [27]. Tart, a scientist who tested him, said he "works very hard to try to 'tell it like it is'" [27]. Russell reports that Monroe gave the proceeds from selling his airline to his dead partner's widow [1].

**Flow State Research cross-reference.** The companion article `/workspace/flow-state-research/something-working-through-them.md` (sourcing: `/workspace/flow-state-research/verification-log.md`) [41] cites Monroe's *Journeys* OBE account as one "transcendent" account of creativity, beside Blake, Ramanujan, Flanagan, and Cayce. That article covers the phenomenology; this report does not repeat it.

## (e) Counter-steelman

- **Inflation of the record.** The most striking claims ("400 programs," "composed all of the orchestral music," "still in use in … motion pictures," "28 weekly," "Director" of Mutual) trace back to Monroe's own CV [3][6] or to sources that drew on it. None of these has independent confirmation, and one claim from the authorized biography, Name That Tune, is contradicted [22–24]. That pattern argues for caution about the rest, including the humility evidence, since much of it is also self-description [25][28].
- **The scientific record.** Meta-analyses of binaural beats are mixed. Garcia-Argibay et al. (2019) pooled 22 studies and found a medium overall effect (g = 0.45) on memory, attention, anxiety, and pain [33]. Ingendoh et al. (2023) reviewed 14 EEG studies: five supported entrainment, eight contradicted it, one was mixed, so it "cannot provide an unequivocal answer" [34], a direct problem for the mechanism the Hemi-Sync name implies. Anesthesia trials also split. Kliempt (1999) [36a] and Lewis (2004) [36b] reported less fentanyl use, while Dabu-Bondoc (2003) found "Hemisync sounds do not reduce the hypnotic component" of anesthesia [36c]. WIRED (2025) summarizes that "there is still an absence of scientific evidence to support the institute's methods" [37].
- **OBE evidence.** Monroe never read a lab target number [27]; TMI's own trainer reported no perfect ten-number result [5].
- **Sleep-learning context.** Monroe's early sleep-learning research [1][30] came after Simon & Emmons (1956), which concluded that "learning during real sleep is … impractical and probably impossible" [35]. Russell reports that Monroe's own sleep-learning trials on eleven subjects were "not sufficiently conclusive to warrant further action" [1].
- **Institutional stake.** TMI sells programs today ($2,695 in-person, $1,150 virtual; 12,500 Gateway Voyage participants since 2022) [37]. Much of the biography comes from TMI or Russell, so independent sources should take priority.

## (f) An honest look at the Disney comparison

Walt Disney holds the individual records for Academy Award wins (22) and nominations (59), 26 Oscars in all; made *Steamboat Willie* (1928) and *Snow White* (1937); opened Disneyland in 1955 to a televised audience of about 70 million; and has eight films in the National Film Registry [38]. I found no comparable awards, surviving canon, or institutional legacy in entertainment for Monroe. His verified broadcast record is that of a successful, versatile **network radio packager and executive**: author of an NBC serial, producer-scorer of a network drama, Mutual programming VP, station and cable owner [11][15][17][21][26]. His lasting cultural reach comes from his *second* career, through *Journeys* and Gateway [27][37].

**Verdict.** A "Disney-level" claim does not hold for broadcasting. The fair comparison class is other radio packagers and music-show producers, such as Harry Salter, Name That Tune's documented creator [23][24]. A defensible framing: a real golden-age radio practitioner whose sound-design instincts carried into a second, more influential and more contested career. Juan may prefer the larger comparison; K-OS-III should present it as his interpretation.
