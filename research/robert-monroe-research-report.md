**How to read the sources.** *Primary* means Monroe's books and patents, period trade press, declassified Army files, tax filings, trademark records, and newspaper clippings. Many clippings survive only through Monroe Institute (TMI) archive uploads, which TMI selected and dated. *Secondary* mainly means Ronald Russell's *The Journey of Robert Monroe* (2007) [1], which draws on Monroe's taped reminiscences and is closely tied to TMI, an institution with a stake in his reputation. Company histories from TMI [9][42] and from Hemi-Sync/Interstate Industries [43] are interested parties. The biographical sheet in the CIA file [3] reads as a résumé Monroe or TMI supplied. Anything sourced only to Monroe, TMI, or Russell is treated as **self-reported** until independently confirmed.

## (a) Early life

**Birthplace.** Monroe was born on **30 October 1915 in Wabash, Indiana**. A 1984 Army information paper [2], a Who's Who–style résumé [6], and Russell [1, pp. 37–38] agree; Russell explains that all five children were born in their mother's family home in Wabash. The Psi Encyclopedia [7] and a Lucid Mind Center biography in TMI's archive [25] say Lexington, Kentucky, where the family *lived* (his father taught Romance languages at Transylvania College [1]). The Psi Encyclopedia cites Russell, so its claim contradicts its own source.

**Family.** His father, Robert Emmett Monroe, was a language professor; his mother, Georgia Helen Jordan, qualified in medicine in 1906 and played cello [1]. The brief's "third of four children" needs correcting: Russell lists five births (Dorothy 1909, Margaret 1911, Robert 1915, Georgia Helen 1919, who died before age two, and Emmett Paul 1923), so Bob was third of five, or third of four who survived infancy [1].

**College.** At Ohio State he moved from engineering to journalism [3]. Russell says he flunked out while hospitalized with burns, spent about a year as a hobo, then finished in English while working on campus radio and theater [1]. The degree is a 1937 B.A. [6][9]. I found no support for the "pre-med" claim that circulates online.

## (b) Broadcasting: the steelman, item by item

The strongest honest version of the owner's position: Monroe went from Ohio station writer to network serial author, packager-producer, national network programming chief, and station and cable owner.

**Early jobs (1937–39). SELF-REPORTED.** WHK Cleveland and WLW Cincinnati per Russell [1]; "Writer and dir. of radio programs, 1937-40" per the résumé [6].

**Rocky Gordon (NBC, c. 1940–43). VERIFIED.** *Radio-Craft*'s November 1940 cover feature shows "Robert Monroe, author of N.B.C.'s dramatic serial 'Rocky Gordon,'" recording locomotives for sound effects [11]. A 1943 clipping lists it weekdays at 5 p.m. on NBC [12].

**Scramble (NBC Blue, c. 1942–43). CONFLICTING.** *Broadcasting* (7 Sept 1942) confirms the show [13]. Two 1943 clippings call Monroe its "originator, writer, director, and even actor" [12]. Neal Thompson's Ripley biography credits Ripley's manager Doug Storer and Ripley [14].

**Company dates. CONFLICT.** The résumé gives Robert Monroe Productions 1946–49 and RAM Enterprises 1950–54 [6]; Russell puts RAM in 1943 [1]; Wikipedia [8] and the Hemi-Sync company history [43] give 1953.

**High Adventure. VERIFIED.** *Radio Daily* (17 Jan 1950), on the NBC run sponsored by Shulton: "Program will be written, directed, produced and musically scored by Robert Monroe" [15]. Run dates per Haendiges [18].

**Nightmare (Peter Lorre). PARTIALLY VERIFIED.** *Radio Daily* (23 Feb 1952): "Bob Monroe mulling over idea of filming Peter Lorre's 'Nightmare' series now on Mutual radio" [16].

**John Steele, Adventurer (Mutual, 1949–). VERIFIED (secondary log).** "Producer: Robert Monroe"; music by Sylvan Levin [17].

**Take a Number / Meet Your Match / MGM Screen Test / Starlight Theatre. SELF-REPORTED.** These appear only on Monroe's own list [3] and in Russell [1].

**"400 programs," "28 shows weekly." SELF-REPORTED.** The CIA-file sheet: *"the creator and producer of some 400 radio and TV network programs in the ensuing twenty year period … he composed all of the orchestral music for his programs, much of which is still in use in television and motion pictures"* [3]. The peak count conflicts: 28 weekly [3], 19 weekly [1][10], 28 monthly [8][43].

**Music. ONE PERIOD CREDIT.** Beyond the 1950 *High Adventure* scoring credit [15], Russell's story of recording a cue library in Cuba to avoid the musicians' union is unverified [1, p. 74]. The Copyright Office records I found are 1980s Hemi-Sync/Metamusic works [39].

**Name That Tune. CONTRADICTED.** Russell credits Monroe [1, pp. 73–74]; Wikipedia, the NYPL, and the University of Wyoming's American Heritage Center all identify Harry Salter as creator [22–24]. Monroe's own lists omit it [3][6][10]. **K-OS-III should not repeat it.**

**Mutual vice president. VERIFIED (c. 1955–56).** The 1956 *Broadcasting Yearbook* lists "Robert Monroe, vp. in charge of prog." [21]; the 1955 edition has someone else [20]. He is not on the 1956 board list [21], so "Director" (repeated by the Hemi-Sync company history as "member of the Board of Directors" [43]) rests on his own claims [3][6].

**Stations and cable. LARGELY VERIFIED.** A 1961 item reports a Monroe-headed corporation buying WEZL (renamed WRGM) and calls him owner of a Durham station [12]. Jefferson Cable clippings show Charlottesville built in early 1963, Waynesboro in 1964, 90% sold to a finance company in 1965, and Monroe still quoted as president at the April 1975 sale to the Mansfield (Ohio) Journal Co. [26]. The obituary's "1967" cable date [25] is contradicted.

## (c) The Monroe Institute: "extremely bizarre extracurricular research and development"

The owner's phrase is a fair description of what the record shows: a broadcast executive's private sound lab that turned into a residential consciousness school, a patent-and-trademark product line, and a contractor to Army intelligence.

### c.1 The books and the laboratory tests

*Journeys Out of the Body* (Doubleday, 1971) [27] reports his experiences from 1958, including: *"I smoothly floated up over the bed, and when I willed myself to stop, I did."* *Kirkus* dismissed "a lot of scientific garblese" [29]. Charles Tart's introduction describes eight University of Virginia EEG sessions (1965–66). Monroe had two brief OBEs and "did not report on the target number," though he correctly said the technician was in the corridor with a man (her husband). Tart: "As a parapsychologist, I cannot say that this 'proves'" anything [27].

### c.2 Corporate structure: a research arm inside a media company

The Hemi-Sync company history says that in 1956 Monroe's production firm "created a Research and Development division to study the effects of various sound patterns on human consciousness," applying "the audio production methods used in the firm's commercial activity." It says the company moved to Virginia in 1962, became Monroe Industries, was renamed Interstate Industries in 1985, and that "the research subsidiary was divested and established as an independent non-profit organization, The Monroe Institute, later in 1985" [43]. In Monroe's own undated history, the Institute began in "the Research and Development Division of Monroe Industries, Inc., which at the time (1958)" studied accelerated learning, and "in 1971, the Institute was created" [42]. The lab started as Whistlefield Research Laboratories, later the Monroe Institute of Applied Sciences: "Using the name Monroe was not an ego factor" [28]. Founding dates still conflict: 1971 [42][37], 1972 [27], 1973 [3], 1974 [10].

### c.3 Patents and trademarks: what Hemi-Sync legally is

- **US 3,884,218** (filed 30 Sept 1970 by Monroe Industries; granted 20 May 1975). Claim 1 covers "amplitude modulating the audio signal" of "a familiar, repetitive, pleasing sound" with "a signal approximating a human EEG signal waveshape characteristic of a state of sleep" [30]. **There are no binaural beats.** Russell's description of it as a patent "for the frequency-following response" [1, p. 166] is inaccurate.
- **US 5,213,562** (1993) credits H. W. Dove with discovering binaural beats in 1839 and says Monroe "coined the term frequency following response, or FFR" [31]. **US 5,356,368** (1994): "The inventor has coined the term HEMI-SYNC" [32]. In Monroe's own history, binaural delivery was suggested by "a member of the research team" [42].
- **Trademarks.** HEMI-SYNC was registered to Interstate Industries in 1989 (Reg. 1,569,367) and cancelled in 1997. A new HEMI SYNC design mark was registered in 2018 (Reg. 5,632,385) and is live [49]. METAMUSIC was registered in 1986 [49]. The brand belongs to the for-profit company, not the nonprofit Institute.

### c.4 Programs and Focus levels

TMI's Gateway began after a 1973 invitation to Esalen. Monroe says it was "originally named the M-5000," with "over 1,500 participants" in four years [42]. Focus 10 is "Mind Awake-Body Asleep" [42]. The Army's 1983 assessment describes Focus 12 as expanded awareness, Focus 15 as "travel into the past" ("probably less than five percent" of participants fully achieve it), and Focus 21 as "the future" [5]. Later programs extend the scale. **Lifeline** ("conceived … in 1991") covers Focus 23–27, including "retrieving" the dead and visiting "The Park," which TMI calls "an afterlife experience reception center" [46]. **Exploration 27** uses Focus 27 "as a springboard for visiting Focus 34 and Focus 35," where participants may "connect with nonphysical intelligences" [48]. Guidelines and Starlines appear in Russell's program history [1].

### c.5 Contact claims, in Monroe's words

- **Locale II** is "a non-material environment" containing "all of the aspects we attribute to heaven and hell," "inhabited … by entities with various degrees of intelligence" [27].
- **Locale III** is "a physical-matter world almost identical to our own" [27].
- **INSPECS** guided him: "short for 'intelligent species,' which presumes humans are not quite so" [28].
- **The Park**: "my one visit in 1965 to what I then called the Park," which he later described as "a construct started by human minds far earlier than our recorded history" [47].

All of this is **first-person testimony with no independent verification**. The report presents it as claims, not findings.

### c.6 The Army: interest and contracting

- **1977.** Lt. F. Holmes "Skip" Atwater, recently assigned to Fort Meade's remote-viewing unit, visited Whistlefield and "concealed the purpose of his visit" [1, pp. 170–172].
- **April 1978.** Army "activity with MIAS techniques" began [4]. INSCOM dates its association to 1978 [2].
- **1980.** A National Agency Check was completed on 18 January 1980 [2], and a SECRET clearance was granted on 11 September 1980 [4].
- **1982–83.** Advanced training of source personnel began in March 1982 [4]. Joseph McMoneagle attended Gateway with Army permission in June 1982, and the unit then "contracted with Monroe" for one-on-one training [1, p. 238]. Monroe "has been paid $24,400.00" for training to begin in fall 1983 [4].
- **9 June 1983. McDonnell's assessment.** Lt. Col. Wayne McDonnell wrote to his commander: "You tasked me to provide an assessment." He drew on "the biomedical models developed by Itzhak Bentov," quantum mechanics, and a "universal hologram" model [5]. Page 25, missing from the declassified copy, was uploaded by TMI's archive account in April 2021 [54]. It links the hologram to the "Holy Spirit" and calls Gateway "a portal" toward "truth in the larger sense" [54]. His conclusion: "a sound, rational basis in terms of physical science parameters for considering Gateway to be plausible" [5]. The same document reports, from a TMI trainer, that no subject asked to read ten numbers out of body ever got all ten [5].
- **5 Jan 1984.** An INSCOM information paper for the Secretary of the Army summarizes the relationship [2].

McDonnell did not stop at plausibility. He proposed "a phased approach" so the Army could establish "an organization-wide exploitation of Gateway's potential" [5]. That is a recommendation, and I found no record in these files that it was adopted at that scale.

These are Army (INSCOM) records released through the CIA reading room. The CIA did not author them.

### c.7 TMI today

The Monroe Institute is a 501(c)(3) at 365 Roberts Mountain Rd, Faber, Virginia (EIN 90-0681223; current exemption issued June 2011, so the present entity postdates the 1985 nonprofit, for reasons I did not establish) [45]. FY2025 Form 990 (filed 18 May 2026): revenue $7,694,796, of which 70.0% came from program services and $1,367,242 (17.8%) from net inventory sales; expenses $7,295,863; total assets $6,538,658 [45]. A Research Division exists under Ross Dunseath [53].

#### c.9.2 His own money: what the record supports

- Russell: Monroe kept OBE-research funding "separate from his cable interests" and tried to raise it through a prefab-housing company that failed [1, pp. 91–92]. He built the Whistlefield lab from that company's leftover materials [1, pp. 140–141], and "at his own expense, devoted almost a decade" to the audio work [1, p. 176]. To buy Roberts Mountain (about $600,000 plus land) he sold the estate, his apartments, and "most of his remaining interest in Jefferson Cable" [1, p. 214].
- 1986 profile: after "more than a decade of pumping his own money into the institute" (the reporter's words), Monroe adds: "I'm not poor; I remember paying in a 90 percent tax bracket" [44].
- Far Journeys: the lab "opened on an entirely voluntary basis"; "all of us were employed in other occupations" [28].

**Complications.** (1) The "own money" was largely *corporate* money. The research began as a division of his commercial production company and stayed a subsidiary of Monroe Industries/Interstate until 1985 [43]. (2) Other people's money was involved. The cable business that made him rich was partly financed by a $400,000 insurance-company loan [1]. Program fees from 1973 onward [42] predate any Army money, and volunteers donated labor [28]. (3) Some outside money came at about the same time as the first Army contact (1977–79), not before it. During construction at Faber, an anonymous donor paid for the Residential Center and then withdrew, and a $15,000-a-unit limited-partnership offering failed [1, pp. 216–217].

**Verdict:** The record **supports "own money first, government later."** Every documented government contact (1977–84) comes after about two decades of self- and company-funded work. The order is solid. "Own funds" means personal *and corporate* funds, plus participant fees.

#### c.9.3 Was the first experience unsought?

Partly. Monroe says his "only unorthodox activity" in 1958 was "experimentation with techniques of data learning during sleep—with myself as the chief subject." After one tape session came the cramp; "it occurred to me that perhaps some factor in the recording had caused it." He stopped the tapes, so for the first vibration three weeks later "there was nothing that apparently triggered the event" [27]. The OBEs were not *sought*, but by his own account they arose **inside his own sound R&D**. That ties the second career directly to the first.

**Couch vs. bed:** there is no real conflict. The vibrations began on "the couch in the living room"; the first separation, floating against the ceiling after thinking of flying "a glider," happened in bed [27]. The obituary (quoting a 1988 interview) gives only the bed-and-glider version, set in Westchester County [25].

**His stated reasons.** Fear came first: "epilepsy," "a brain disorder such as a tumor"; he was "much more concerned about the possibility of a growing mental illness" [27]. He saw his physician, Dr. Richard Gordon, and the psychologist Dr. Foster Bradshaw [27]. Later he wrote that he researched "out of self-preservation and, as the fear and panic subsided, out of growing curiosity" [28]. Bradshaw told him: "there's nothing else you can do but look into it and see what it is." Monroe added that he "couldn't afford to go off in the woods, either literally or figuratively. I had a family to support" [27]. That is the opposite of a clean break from business. A 1986 profile reports it took him a year to convince himself he was not going out of his mind [44]. The same profile complicates the "purest truth" reading: it reports that about ten years earlier he "tired of" out-of-body travel because sightseeing "soon became routine" (the reporter's paraphrase) [44]. By the time he built the Faber campus, his personal exploration had given way to building programs.

#### c.9.4 Did entertainment seem "cheap" to him?

**I found no explicit statement** in *Journeys*, *Far Journeys*, the 1986 profile, the obituary, or Russell that commercial radio felt shallow or insufficient. "Cheap" remains the **owner's reading**. The nearest material points elsewhere. Monroe called his business standing an asset: being "well grounded and active in the material world of business helped greatly" [27]. He hid his OBEs because his board and stockholders might balk [44]. Late in the 1970s he disliked being "the rich guy on the hill," but that was about the estate, not about show business [1, p. 213]. Tom Campbell judged he wanted "legitimacy rather than financial reward or public recognition" [1], which loosely supports a turn away from *attention*. Psychiatrists Twemlow and Gabbard, who evaluated him in the late 1970s, wondered how he could accept "these highly esoteric journeys … and at the same time be the almost traditional businessman and father" who "does not put himself constantly on-stage" [1, p. 175]. That supports the owner's humility point, but it describes a dual life, not a renunciation.

**Counter: overlap, not a clean break.** He remained a media executive for 17 years after 1958. He was Jefferson Cable's president until 1975 [26] and listed Southern States Radio through 1975 [6]; Russell instead says he sold the stations to fund cable [1], a conflict. He commercialized the research through books [27][28], patents [30–32], trademarks [49], tapes sold by Monroe Industries [43], and paid programs [42].

#### c.9.5 "Intervention" or "interest and contracting"?

**"Interest and contracting" fits the record better.** The Army approached him (Atwater), sent personnel to public programs, cleared him, and paid a defined training fee [2][4]. No document I found shows the government directing or redirecting the Institute's agenda, or funding it beyond the training fee. Some caveats count against the owner's "not sanctioned" theme: Monroe held a SECRET clearance, the Army paid him, and McDonnell recommended "organization-wide exploitation" [5].

## (d) The owner's framing *(Juan's interpretation, not historical consensus)*

Juan accepts that Monroe never matched Disney's scale. He rejects scale as the measure of worth and argues that Monroe is an **underground, underrated legend**: a working artist of golden-age radio, personally humble, who used his commercial toolkit for independent consciousness research. The best evidence:

- **Craft across roles.** Period proof shows him writing, directing, producing, and scoring one network show [15], and recording field sound [11].
- **One career, two halves.** Russell calls the radio music cues "a foretaste of … Metamusic" [1, p. 74]. The 1958 OBEs came out of his sound R&D (c.9.3).
- **Independent R&D.** He spent his own and company money for about two decades before any government contact (c.9.2).
- **Underground reach.** Decades later, a 2025 WIRED headline says Gateway has "never been more popular" [37]. McDonnell's paper and TMI's recovered page 25 are both freely available online [5][54].
- **Humility.** "Not an ego factor" [28]; "I discount very highly the appellation of guru" [25]; "I was a conventional person, convinced of conventional reality" [1]. Tart said he "works very hard to try to 'tell it like it is'" [27].

**Flow State cross-reference.** `/workspace/flow-state-research/something-working-through-them.md` [41] cites *Journeys* among its "transcendent" accounts of creativity.

## (e) Counter-steelman

- **"Underrated" is hard to test.** By what measure? TMI says his legacy has touched "literally millions" [42], and a 2025 WIRED headline says Gateway has "never been more popular" [37]. On that evidence he is well *known*, just not academically *credited*.
- **Not a lone outsider.** He had capital, a 90% tax bracket [44], a company, a brand, a patent law firm [30], and a SECRET clearance [2][4].
- **Inflation.** "400 programs," "all of the orchestral music," and "28 weekly" trace back to his own CV [3][6]. Name That Tune is false [22–24]. Much of the humility evidence is also self-description.
- **Science.** Binaural-beat meta-analyses are mixed: a medium pooled effect (g = 0.45) [33], but EEG entrainment is unsupported in 8 of 14 studies [34]. Anesthesia trials split [36a–c]. WIRED: "an absence of scientific evidence to support the institute's methods" [37].
- **OBE evidence.** He never read a lab target number [27]. Contact claims (c.5) are unverifiable.
- **Sleep learning.** Simon & Emmons (1956) had already called it "impractical and probably impossible" [35]; his own 11-subject trial was inconclusive [1].
- **Commerce.** The "research" is mostly a paid program and product business [45].

## (f) An honest look at the Disney comparison, and the "underground legend" alternative

Disney holds the individual records for Academy Award wins (22) and nominations (59), made *Snow White* (1937), and opened Disneyland in 1955 [38]. I found no comparable awards, canon, or entertainment institution for Monroe. His verified broadcasting record is that of a versatile **network radio packager and executive** [11][15][17][21][26].

**Verdict.** The owner's revised frame is more defensible than a Disney-scale claim. The question is no longer "as big as Disney?" but "more significant than his reputation suggests?" On that, the record gives real support: a documented arc from radio sound craft to corporate sound R&D, an accidental 1958 experience inside that R&D, two decades of self-funded work, and later Army contracting. It also gives real caveats: inflated credits, weak scientific support, and a commercial enterprise. K-OS-III can call him an "underground figure" with confidence. "Legend" and "underrated" should be attributed to Juan.
