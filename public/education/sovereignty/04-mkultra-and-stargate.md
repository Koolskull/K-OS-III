# MKUltra, Stargate, and declassified intelligence programs

There is a particular silence in American history: the silence after the shredder. Boxes gone. Then, years later, a committee room, a microphone, and the country learning—again—that secret programs can injure the people they claim to protect.

{{illust:file-shred|Paper strips falling forever — what 1973 destruction feels like in motion.}}

## Short version

- **MKUltra** (and related behavioral research lines) is documented through Church Committee materials, Inspector General reporting, FOIA releases, and collections such as the National Security Archive—not through vibes alone.
- Large behavioral files were destroyed in 1973 under orders associated with then-DCI Richard Helms / OTS—this is part of why the record is incomplete.
- **Stargate** and related remote-viewing efforts are a later, partially declassified story. The 1995 American Institutes for Research evaluation found the product too vague for actionable intelligence and did not support continuation.

Documented abuse and bureaucratic failure are real. That does **not** make every internet legend a footnote of MKUltra.

{{illust:church-committee|A long table, microphones, seats that never quite sit still — oversight as a scene.}}

## A narrative of two eras

**Act I — Behavioral control research.** Cold War fear made human subjects into terrain. Drugs, isolation, sensory games, front organizations—the public record is ugly enough without embellishment. When the shredders ran, they did not erase the ethical stain; they only made the map harder to complete.

**Act II — Anomalous cognition / remote viewing.** A different appetite: *can we see what satellites cannot?* Programs changed names and sponsors. Evaluators argued. One tradition emphasizes occasional statistical curiosities; the operational review that closed the door emphasized **actionable uselessness**.

### Reading order that keeps you sane

1. Church Committee / official summaries of intelligence abuses.
2. National Security Archive curated collections on CIA behavioral research.
3. AIR 1995 evaluation of remote viewing (and Jessica Utts’s separate statistical view, labeled as such).
4. Popular books (*Poisoner in Chief*, etc.) as **secondary narrative**, not the FOIA stack itself.

## Sources

- Church Committee reports (1975–76).
- National Security Archive MKUltra / behavioral research collections.
- CIA Reading Room FOIA releases.
- AIR evaluation materials on Stargate-era remote viewing (1995).

**How to use:** When a claim says "MKUltra did X," ask which **document** says X. If the answer is a meme, demote the claim.
