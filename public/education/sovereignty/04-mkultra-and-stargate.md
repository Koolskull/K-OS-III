# MKUltra, Stargate, and declassified intelligence programs

## Short version

Two clusters of U.S. intelligence activity are now partially declassified and well documented:

**MKUltra** (and related earlier efforts under names such as Bluebird and Artichoke) was a CIA program, roughly 1953–1973, that tested drugs (especially LSD), hypnosis, sensory deprivation, and other techniques for interrogation and behavior influence. Many subjects were unwitting. Large portions of the records were destroyed in 1973. The Church Committee (1975–76) and subsequent releases established the basic facts of illegal domestic experimentation and ethical violations.

**Stargate** (and predecessor programs such as Grill Flame, Center Lane, and Sun Streak) was a series of Defense Intelligence / Army / CIA efforts, primarily 1970s–1995, that investigated remote viewing for intelligence collection. An external evaluation by the American Institutes for Research in 1995 concluded that while some statistical anomalies appeared, the material was not operationally actionable. The program was terminated. Substantial files have been declassified.

These are real, documented cases of overreach and secrecy. They demonstrate that intelligence agencies have conducted unethical human experimentation and have pursued fringe methods when they believed national security required it. They do not, by themselves, prove a single continuous cabal directing all later events.

## Expanded notes

### MKUltra

Allen Dulles authorized the expanded program in 1953. Sidney Gottlieb, a CIA chemist, was central to the drug and behavioral work. Experiments occurred in universities, hospitals, prisons, and safehouses. Front organizations funded research without always informing the investigators of the ultimate sponsor. The 1973 destruction order by CIA Director Richard Helms severely limited later investigation; surviving documents and testimony still confirm the core activities.

Primary places to read:

- Church Committee reports (Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities).
- National Security Archive collections on CIA behavioral research and MKUltra.
- Surviving declassified CIA files released in later decades.

Mainstream historical accounts treat MKUltra as a Cold War excess driven by fear of Soviet and Chinese brainwashing techniques, combined with institutional secrecy and weak internal constraints. Dissenting or expanded accounts sometimes link it to longer traditions of behavioral control or to later, less documented programs. The verified core is the Church Committee record and the surviving files; broader claims require separate evidence.

### Stargate / remote viewing

The programs arose in part from concern that the Soviet Union was investing in psychic research. Remote viewers were tasked against various intelligence targets. Results were mixed; some individual sessions were later claimed as hits by participants, while the formal 1995 evaluation found the overall product insufficient for operational use. The files are public.

Primary places to read:

- CIA Reading Room Stargate collection.
- American Institutes for Research evaluation (1995).
- Participant memoirs (e.g., Joseph McMoneagle) as first-person accounts, to be weighed against the formal evaluation.

### How to read this material

Treat the declassified record as established fact. Treat interpretations that connect every subsequent intelligence or cultural phenomenon to these programs as hypotheses that still need evidence. The existence of real abuse does not automatically validate every later claim of continuous control. The reverse is also true: the termination of a named program does not prove that related research or techniques simply vanished.

## Sources

- Church Committee reports (U.S. Senate, 1975–76).
- National Security Archive, "CIA and the Behavioral Sciences" collections.
- CIA Reading Room, Stargate / remote viewing releases.
- American Institutes for Research, evaluation of the remote viewing program (1995).
- Secondary historical treatments (e.g., Stephen Kinzer, *Poisoner in Chief*) that rely on the declassified record.
