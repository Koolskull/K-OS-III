*Research report for KOOLSKULL / K-OS education track. Compiled 6 October 2026 (PT). Part of the "flow state / something working through them" research set.*

Whenever a sentence is my own reasoning and not a source's, I say so. Every source, with its URL, is in `THEREMIN_SOURCES.md`, and each claim is logged with the source I actually checked in `theremin-verification-log.md`.

## Part 1: Leon Theremin, a life checked against the record

### Birth, training, and the invention (1896–1922)

Lev Sergeyevich Termen, known in the West as Leon Theremin, was born in St. Petersburg in 1896 **[VERIFIED]**. The exact date is a small trap. The Sakharov Center's Gulag memoir archive, drawing on Galeyev, gives "15(28) August 1896": 15 August in the old Julian calendar and 28 August in the modern one. In a 2016 ThereminWorld piece, Lydia Kavina explains that in 1896 the gap between the calendars was 12 days, not 13. Strictly, then, 15 August Old Style converts to 27 August. Theremin himself celebrated on 28 August, and that date was the one that became official. Wikipedia gives a third version, 16 August Old Style. **[DISPUTED: 27 vs 28 August New Style. Recommend writing "born 15 August 1896 (Old Style); his birthday was observed on 28 August."]**

He studied cello at the Petrograd Conservatory. He told Mattis he graduated as a "free artist on the violoncello" **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: his own account, consistent with the Gulag archive's mention of cello studies in 1916]**. He also studied physics. By 1919 he was running a laboratory at Abram Ioffe's Physical-Technical Institute **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: Gulag archive and Nesturkh]**.

The instrument's origin date is cleaner than most sources make it look. Theremin's own paper states: "I designed the first musical instrument of this type and demonstrated it at the meeting of the Mechanical Engineers Group named after Professor Kirpichev at the Petersburg Polytechnic Institute in November 1920." **[VERIFIED: primary source]** Fischer's CIA article says the instrument was "invented in 1919", and some popular sources repeat that. My best reading, which is an inference: the lab work that produced it began in 1919, and the instrument existed and was demonstrated by November 1920. **[DISPUTED: 1919 vs 1920; Theremin's own text supports 1920.]**

**Correction to the brief: there is no "1925 paper."** The founding text is "The Design of a Musical Instrument Based on Cathode Relays." Theremin delivered it as a public lecture at the State Institute of Musical Science on 11 November 1921, published it in 1922 (signed "Petrograd 28 May 1922"), and *Leonardo Music Journal* printed it in English translation in 1996 (vol. 6, pp. 49–50, translated by Oleg Petrishev) **[CORRECTED; VERIFIED from the article and Crossref metadata, DOI 10.2307/1513305]**. The paper is short and still clear. It explains the beat principle with numbers: two oscillations of 1,000,000 and 1,000,100 per second produce an audible "interference tone" of 100. It notes that "a very small change (0.01% or, even, 0.001%) of electrical capacitance" is enough to sweep that tone across the musical range. It also states his artistic aim: "I believe that the movement of the hand in space, unencumbered by pressing a string or bow, is capable of performing in an extremely delicate manner." **[VERIFIED quotations]**

### Lenin and the Soviet launch (1922)

In 1922 Theremin demonstrated the instrument to Lenin in the Kremlin **[VERIFIED: Theremin's own account to Mattis, plus Galeyev; the Gulag archive gives March 1922]**. In the Mattis interview, Theremin described the demonstration in detail, including playing Glinka's "Skylark" for Lenin. These details come from his own memory, recalled 67 years later. **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: single first-person source]**

### Patent, America, and fame (1924–1938)

The US patent is solid. US Patent 1,661,058, "Method of and apparatus for the generation of sounds," lists "Leo Ssergejewitsch Theremin" of Leningrad as inventor, with assignment to M. J. Goldberg und Söhne of Berlin. It was filed 5 December 1925, claims German priority from 8 December 1924, and was granted 28 February 1928, with 40 claims **[VERIFIED: Google Patents]**. The patent's own wording matters for physics teaching. It describes oscillators near 500,000 cycles per second, chosen "that no interference with radio broadcast reception is produced". It also says the playing electrode "does not receive or detect radiant energy." The antenna, in other words, is not working as a radio antenna.

He reached New York in late December 1927. The *New York Times* of 22 December 1927 ran "Inventor to Exhibit 'Ether Music' Here; Russian Comes to Demonstrate…," and Mattis's interview notes give his arrival as 22 December 1927 **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: NYT headline only; article body not accessible]**. The "30 December" date found online is not supported by what I checked.

**Toscanini and Rachmaninoff: [VERIFIED, with an important clarification.]** The famous audience was at a *private* demonstration in the ballroom of the Hotel Plaza on the evening of 24 January 1928, not at his first public concert. The *NYT* headline of 25 January 1928 reads: "Ether Concert Stirs Musical Stars Here; Rachmaninoff, Kreisler and Toscanini Hear Program by Russian Inventor…" The *Brooklyn Daily Eagle* of the same day (transcribed online) and Robert A. Simon's column in the *New Yorker* (dated 28 January 1928) name the same musicians. The first *public* concert came on 31 January 1928 at the Metropolitan Opera House. The *NYT* covered it on 1 February with the headline "'Ether Wave' Concert; Prof. Theremin Gives First Public Demonstration," and *Musical America* (4 February 1928, quoted in the *New Yorker* in 1990) said the program repeated the Plaza one. Whether Toscanini or Rachmaninoff also attended the Met concert is **[UNVERIFIED]**.

In 1929 RCA began producing a licensed theremin. The best count I could check is about 500 instruments: about 300 built by General Electric and 200 by Westinghouse, numbered 1–500, per RCATheremin.com. Musicologist Kelly Hiser, writing for New Music USA, says RCA "sold only 485" at about $230 each. Mark Brend, writing for Reverb, says about 500 were made and roughly 135 survive **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: consistent secondary sources; no RCA sales ledger seen]**. One survives at the Metropolitan Museum of Art (accession "Purchase, Amati Gifts, 2017") **[VERIFIED: Met collection record]**.

In New York he trained performers, the most important being Clara Rockmore (born Vilnius, 9 March 1911; died Manhattan, 10 May 1998, per the Library of Congress authority file) **[VERIFIED]**. He also trained the patron and performer Lucie Bigelow Rosen **[VERIFIED: Caramoor's history timeline plus Theremin's own account]**. He built the Rhythmicon, an early rhythm machine. The Smithsonian holds one made by Theremin in New York around 1931 and used by the composer Joseph Schillinger **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: Smithsonian page blocked by a bot check; seen only as a search snippet]**. Theremin told Mattis he married the African-American dancer Lavinia Williams and that the Soviet consulate had earlier demanded he divorce his first wife, Katia **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: his own account; the ACO's 2025 article also says he left Williams behind in 1938]**. A ten-theremin ensemble played Carnegie Hall, but the year varies by source: Fischer says 1928, Galeyev 1929, and the Wikipedia article on Rosen 1930 **[DISPUTED]**.

### The 1938 departure: kidnapped or voluntary? [DISPUTED]

This is the biggest contested claim in his life, and the brief should not present either version as settled. Here are the accounts, by name:

- **Kidnapping version.** Galeyev reports that Martin's 1993 film "informs us… that Theremin was kidnapped by the KGB and returned to the Soviet Union by force in 1938." Robert Moog's 1996 *Electronic Musician* sidebar says he "was taken back to Russia by Soviet agents under circumstances that are still not fully known." Fischer writes that friends in New York believed he had been kidnapped.
- **Voluntary version.** Galeyev, in the same article: "Theremin told me that he made the decision to leave the United States himself, being anxious about the impending war." To Mattis in 1989, Theremin said he had asked to be sent home and sailed as an assistant to a ship's captain, and that his wife was not allowed to follow. In 1967 he told Harold Schonberg of the *NYT* that he returned because "his country was in trouble."
- **Intelligence-and-debt version.** Fischer, citing Russian sources, says Theremin had worked for Soviet military intelligence (GRU) since arriving. He says Theremin left in September 1938 aboard a Soviet vessel as a crew member under an assumed name, with GRU help, while "one step ahead of" US immigration, tax, and labor authorities over business debts. Galeyev independently describes a "double task" of business and intelligence from 1928.

Galeyev himself warns that Theremin told different versions over the years. My inference: these accounts are not fully contradictory. Theremin may have chosen to leave under pressure from debts and his handlers, in a way his New York friends experienced as a disappearance. The exact sailing date and ship are **[UNVERIFIED]**. Note that Fischer says Theremin told his Russian biographer about the decision "in 1995", two years after Theremin died. That is a dating error in Fischer.

### Arrest, Kolyma, and the sharashka (1939–1947)

He was arrested in Moscow in March 1939 **[VERIFIED: Fischer, Gulag archive]**. The sentence is **[DISPUTED]**: eight years according to the Gulag archive and Theremin's own statement to Mattis that he was held for eight years, six years according to Fischer. He was sent to the Kolyma region, transported via Magadan **[VERIFIED: Glinsky p. 259 via the scan, Fischer, Gulag archive]**. What he did there is **[DISPUTED]**: Fischer places him at a gold-mining camp, while Theremin (to Mattis) and the Gulag archive describe road and construction work, with him as a brigade foreman. Fischer also wrongly puts Magadan above the Arctic Circle; it lies on the Sea of Okhotsk. That point is my own geographic check, not a source claim. He spent about eight months there by his own account and nine months by Glinsky's. Then he was moved into the prison design bureau TsKB-29, where Andrei Tupolev was also held, and later to the Kuchino sharashka **[VERIFIED: multiple sources]**. A *sharashka* was a prison laboratory where convict scientists worked on state projects.

### The Great Seal bug and Buran: the brief conflates two devices [CORRECTED]

The brief links Theremin's Stalin Prize to "The Thing", the bug hidden in the Great Seal. The sources describe two different devices.

1. **"The Thing" / Great Seal bug (1945).** This was a passive resonant-cavity microphone hidden in a carved wooden Great Seal that Soviet Young Pioneers presented to US Ambassador Averell Harriman. It had no battery and no wiring. A radio beam aimed from outside energized it, and sound vibrating a membrane modulated the re-radiated signal **[VERIFIED: NSA National Cryptologic Museum; Glinsky pp. 260–261; Fischer]**. The presentation date is **[DISPUTED]**: Glinsky and Fischer say 4 July 1945, the NSA exhibit says 4 August 1945. It was discovered in 1952 **[VERIFIED]**. The NSA's "The Thing and I" article credits a technician named Joseph Bezjian with the find. In May 1960, after the U-2 shootdown, Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge displayed it at the United Nations **[VERIFIED]**. Fischer says the General Assembly. Galeyev says a Security Council session, and so does the Crypto Museum, which gives the date as 26 May 1960. The Crypto Museum also notes the July/August dispute over the presentation date. The bug's operating frequency is **[DISPUTED]**: Glinsky gives 330 MHz, while the Crypto Museum, citing the FBI's 1952 laboratory report (declassified 2019), gives 1,650–1,800 MHz (about 1.7 GHz). Of the two, the FBI-based figure is the better documented.
2. **Buran (1947).** Glinsky writes that "Beria enlisted Lev Sergeyevich for a second eavesdropping operation in 1947… code-named Buran." It was an infrared beam bounced off a window pane, with the glass's speech-driven vibrations recovered at a distance. Glinsky gives up to 1,600 feet, Galeyev "300–500 m." It didn't work in rain or fog **[VERIFIED: Glinsky via scan, Galeyev, Fischer]**.

The **Stalin Prize, First Class, 1947** is tied to **Buran**, not to the Seal bug **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED]**. Galeyev says so directly. Fischer says the prize was "possibly" for Buran. Secret Stalin Prizes were not published, and I found no official prize list. Galeyev also reports Theremin's chilling answer when asked about surveillance of ordinary people: "I didn't care. My task was to reduce noise when operating the device." **[VERIFIED quote, as reported by Galeyev]**

### Release, the KGB years, and the Conservatory (1947–1967)

He was released in 1947. Galeyev calls this "unbelievable": he "returned voluntarily to the KGB," continuing classified work until about 1966 **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: Galeyev, Fischer, his own account to Mattis]**. The date of his formal **rehabilitation** is **[UNVERIFIED]**: Wikipedia says 1956, Fischer says the "early 1960s," and I found no primary record.

He then set up an electronic-music lab at the Moscow Conservatory. In April 1967 *NYT* critic Harold Schonberg found him there and published a report (26 April 1967, p. 40). It quotes Theremin saying "I made my last public appearance in 1938" and calls him a "professor of acoustics at the Moscow conservatory" **[VERIFIED: full text]**. Nesturkh writes that the article came to the attention of the Conservatory's deputy director, "who decided to discharge Theremin." Theremin recalled the official's view as "electricity is not good for music; electricity is to be used for electrocution." The dismissal date is **[DISPUTED]**: 1967 per Nesturkh, the Gulag archive, and Mattis's correction of Theremin's own memory, against 1971 per Fischer. The weight of evidence favors 1967.

### Moscow State University: a mechanic, not a professor [CORRECTED]

He next worked in the acoustics department of Moscow State University's physics faculty. Nesturkh says he was there "not as a professor, but as a mechanic," and Fischer gives his job as a "grade six mechanic" **[VERIFIED: two independent sources]**. Kavina remembers him coming to her family's apartment every Friday "after working a 40-hour week at the university," while he was teaching her in the late 1970s **[VERIFIED quote]**.

### Late recognition and death (1989–1993)

The brief compresses his travels. **[CORRECTED]** He went first to the Bourges festival in France in June 1989, where Mattis interviewed him on 16 June. He was in Stockholm in 1990 (Fischer) and in the United States in 1991 at Stanford's invitation, followed by New York **[VERIFIED: Mattis, Galeyev, Gulag archive]**. In 1991 he joined the Communist Party, telling Galeyev: "I promised Lenin I would!" **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: Galeyev; the Gulag archive gives March 1991]**. He died in Moscow on 3 November 1993 **[VERIFIED: Gulag archive, Galeyev, NYT obituary headline of 9 November 1993]**. Fischer's claim that Martin's film aired on the BBC "two days before" his death, and in March, is internally inconsistent and **[UNVERIFIED]**.

## Part 2: Historical context

### Before and beside the theremin

The theremin was not the first electrical instrument. It was the first played without touch. Thaddeus Cahill's US Patent 580,035 (granted 6 April 1897) describes generating music electrically and distributing it "from one central station" to listeners elsewhere. That machine became the Telharmonium **[VERIFIED: patent; the later name is common knowledge, not checked]**. Maurice Martenot presented his ondes Martenot at the Paris Opéra (Palais Garnier) on 3 May 1928, per the Philharmonie de Paris archive, which says it grew from his 1919 discovery of the sound of radio valves **[VERIFIED]**. A German broadcasting archive dates the Trautonium's first public performance to 20 June 1930 in Berlin, with Hindemith, Oskar Sala, and Rudolph Schmidt playing **[VERIFIED: Rundfunkschätze; the Deutsches Museum page timed out]**. Laurens Hammond's tonewheel organ patent, US 1,956,350, was filed and granted in 1934 **[VERIFIED: patent; the 1935 commercial launch is not checked]**. A New York Public Library photo shows Ferde Grofé playing a Novachord at the 1939–40 New York World's Fair **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED]**.

Both the ondes Martenot and the theremin grew out of radio-valve oscillators, but the ondes put a keyboard and a ribbon in front of the player. **Author's inference:** the theremin's lack of any reference point is exactly why it stayed a specialist's instrument while keyboard instruments spread.

### The Soviet scene

The theremin had a parallel life in the USSR. Kavina and Nesturkh both name Gavriil ("Gavreil" in Kavina's text) Popov's score for the 1934 film *Komsomol, Patron of Electrification* as the first film score to use it. Konstantin Kovalsky played on that score and later soloed with Vjacheslav Mescherin's Ensemble of Electronic Musical Instruments **[VERIFIED: LMJ articles]**. Nesturkh also names Eugene (Yevgeny) Murzin, "inventor of the ANS synthesizer," and the electronic studio at the Scriabin Museum **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: single source; ANS dates not checked]**.

### Film: the sound of the uncanny

Miklós Rózsa used the theremin, played by Dr. Samuel Hoffman, in *Spellbound* (Brend says Rózsa first called Hoffman in 1944), a score Brend reports won the Academy Award, and in *The Lost Weekend*. Bernard Herrmann's *The Day the Earth Stood Still* used two theremins, with Hoffman on high and Paul Shure on low **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: Brend for Reverb, theremin.info, LA Times 2013 obituary of Paul Tanner; Oscars database not reachable]**. Hoffman also played on *It Came from Outer Space* (1953) **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED: Brend]**.

### Rock: "Good Vibrations" was not a theremin [MISATTRIBUTED in common telling]

The Beach Boys' "Good Vibrations" used the **Electro-Theremin** ("Paul's Box"), built by trombonist Paul Tanner with Bob Whitsell. The FAQ of the Electro-Theremin's own website says plainly: "No. The instrument was not a real, traditional, two antenna-type theremin." It was a sine-wave oscillator controlled by a hand-moved slider, played in a way the *LA Times* calls "closer to that of traditional keyboard instruments" **[VERIFIED]**. Even Fischer's CIA article repeats the error. Jimmy Page did use a real theremin in Led Zeppelin. In a 2014 *Guitar World* interview he said, "I created most of the sounds with a Theremin and my guitar." **[VERIFIED]** The claim that it was a "Sonic Wave" pitch-only model is **[PARTIALLY VERIFIED]**: it rests on a Metropolitan Museum record seen only in a search result, because the page blocked my fetch.

### Robert Moog

Moog's career began with theremins. The Moog Foundation says he built them as a teenager, published his first article, "The Theremin," in *Radio & Television News* in January 1954 at age 19, and sold the Model 201 theremin for $87.95, with only 15–20 made **[VERIFIED: Moog Foundation, plus the January 1954 magazine scan with Moog's byline, schematic, and an R.A. Moog Co. kit advertisement]**. **[CORRECTED]** The brief calls the magazine *Electronics World*. The 1954 title was *Radio & Television News*, and the magazine was renamed later. In February 1996 Moog published "Build the EM Theremin" in *Electronic Musician* **[VERIFIED]**. In 1977 he recorded Clara Rockmore's LP *The Art of the Theremin* **[VERIFIED: Rockmore Foundation; Hiser]**.

### The physics of capacitance, in one paragraph

Your body is a conductor more or less connected to ground. Hold your hand near a metal rod and the hand and rod form a tiny capacitor. Moog: "During normal operation, the hand capacitance is less than one picofarad, which is a very small capacitance indeed!" **[CORRECTED: the brief says "a few picofarads"; Moog says less than one]**. In the instrument, that rod is part of the tuning circuit of a radio-frequency oscillator, so moving your hand shifts the oscillator's frequency very slightly. A second, fixed oscillator runs at almost the same frequency. Mixing the two produces their *difference*, the "beat," which lands in the audible range. A shift of a few hundredths of a percent at hundreds of kilohertz becomes a sweep of several octaves of audio. That is Theremin's 1922 arithmetic exactly. The volume antenna works on a different principle. The hand detunes another oscillator away from a filter's resonance, and the result becomes a control voltage that sets loudness.

### Players in their own words

Each quotation below was verified in a source I opened:

- **Lydia Kavina** (*LMJ*, 1996): "the performer must concentrate intently on the sound, which gives the impression that the musician is holding the sounds in the air… the player often becomes the personification of the music itself." Also: "one can only control the tone by relying on one's own sense of hearing," and "many years of study and practice are required."
- **Clara Rockmore** (Rockmore Foundation biography): "I was fascinated by the aesthetic part of it, the visual beauty, the idea of playing in the air… and I loved the sound."
- **Carolina Eyck** (The World/PRX, 15 Oct 2019): "you have to be very focused to play your notes in tune, but then, you have so much freedom… So, it's really balanced between being focused, being concentrated and using the freedom you have." From *Digital in Berlin*: "every little movement matters, every breath matters. I see this as an advantage, an invitation to be present and to connect to the current moment." To the Australian Chamber Orchestra (2025): "The only thing you feel yourself is your body," and she says she makes sure she is "feeling sort of grounded" before playing.
- **Pamelia Kurstin** (TED transcript, 2002 talk): "I think of it almost like like a yoga instrument because it makes you so aware of every little crazy thing your body is doing," and "you're always relying on your ears and adjusting constantly."

### The flow-state connection [EDITORIAL INFERENCE]

None of these sources uses the phrase "flow state," and none says that "something works through" the player. That framing is mine, offered for the K-OS track, not a sourced claim. What the players do describe, in remarkably consistent terms, are the textbook conditions of absorbed attention. Performance depends on moment-to-moment feedback through the ear alone (Kavina, Kurstin). It takes total stillness and body awareness (Kurstin, Eyck). Concentration and freedom have to be balanced (Eyck). Eyck says it invites her to be present. Kavina's observation that the player "becomes the personification of the music itself" comes closest to the "working through them" idea, but she is describing how the audience perceives the player.

Kavina also supplies a strong counterpoint, and I recommend Juan include it. The theremin "is played spontaneously… its music is created by the musician, not by the instrument itself." On her account the instrument channels nothing on its own. It amplifies the player's attention, and the "magic" is trained skill that takes "many years of study and practice." A fair framing for the track: the theremin removes the physical interface, so the player's focus becomes the interface.

## Part 3: Build tutorial

### (a) Physics you need

You will build three things: (1) a pitch section, a *variable* oscillator whose tank circuit includes the pitch antenna, plus a *fixed* reference oscillator and a mixer/detector that outputs the difference frequency; (2) a volume section, an oscillator detuned by the volume antenna and a detector that turns this into a DC control voltage; and (3) a voltage-controlled amplifier (VCA) that the control voltage drives. In Moog's EM design the pitch antenna circuit resonates near 260 kHz and the volume circuit near 450 kHz, and the audio beat runs from 0 to about 3 kHz **[VERIFIED: Moog 1996]**. The Open.Theremin uses crystal-controlled fixed oscillators and LC variable oscillators, with an Arduino doing the mixing and sound generation digitally **[VERIFIED: Opensource.com, Gaudi.ch]**. PAiA's Theremax kit uses oscillator coils around 796 kHz **[VERIFIED: Theremax manual]**.

### (b) Components and cost

From Moog's 1996 article **[VERIFIED]**:

- general-purpose 2N3904 transistors
- 1N4148 and 1N4001 diodes
- J.W. Miller RF chokes
- Toko adjustable inductors, used for tuning
- an LM13600 transconductance amplifier as the VCA
- LM78L12/LM79L12 regulators fed by a 12 VAC wall transformer, so no mains voltage inside the box
- 3/8-inch copper tube antennas: an 18-inch vertical pitch rod and a 9-inch hairpin loop for volume
- a perfboard (he names a Radio Shack board)

Cost: in 1996 Big Briar sold the complete EM kit for $229 including US shipping **[VERIFIED price, 1996]**. I did **not** verify any current prices on 6 October 2026, so I give none. Specific coils may now be hard to find. That is a caution, not a confirmed fact, so check availability before committing to Moog's exact parts list. For comparison, RCA's 1929 retail price was about $230 (Hiser), and Moog's 1954 kit-era Model 201 was $87.95 (Moog Foundation).

### (d) Assembly

Following Moog's and PAiA's guidance:

- Build and test in stages: power supply first, then each oscillator, then the mixer, then the VCA. Moog's article lists checkout voltages at each stage.
- Solder with a small-tipped iron of no more than 35 W, and use rosin-core solder only, never acid-core. PAiA also warns that soldering guns are "completely unacceptable" for solid-state work **[VERIFIED: Theremax manual]**.
- Mount the antennas with solid mechanical joints. Moog notes how to avoid ground loops between the audio and power grounds.
- **Author's inference:** a non-metallic enclosure, or one with careful shielding, keeps stray capacitance stable.

### (e) Calibration

Calibration is a zero-beat procedure. With your hand away from the pitch antenna, adjust the variable oscillator (Moog: inductor L6, then L5 for range) until the pitch falls to silence, the null point, where the two oscillators match. The Etherwave manual describes the same idea with its PITCH RANGE knob: turn slowly until the tone "stops completely (null point)," then set the playing range with your body in playing position **[VERIFIED: Etherwave 2022 manual; Moog 1996]**. Then set the volume oscillator (Moog: L11) so the instrument goes silent with your hand near the loop. Do this in the room where you'll play. The Etherwave manual warns that "nearby large objects will affect" performance and asks for a non-metallic surface **[VERIFIED]**. The Open.Theremin V4 has an automatic calibration touch button **[VERIFIED: Gaudi.ch]**. Moog's factory manual advises: "Do not attempt calibration unless you understand the procedure."

### (f) Safety

- **Solder fume.** The UK Health and Safety Executive calls rosin-based flux fume "a top cause of occupational asthma." Use fume extraction, never overheat the iron, and "keep your head out of the plume" **[VERIFIED: HSE]**.
- **Lead.** UNC Environment, Health and Safety says "the primary route of exposure to lead from soldering is ingestion due to surface contamination." Use lead-free solder where possible, don't eat or drink at the bench, and wash your hands and arms afterward **[VERIFIED: UNC EHS]**.
- **Burns.** Don't touch the iron's tip or element, and let joints cool **[VERIFIED: UNC EHS]**.
- **Mains power.** The Moog EM design uses a low-voltage 12 VAC wall adapter, and the Etherwave uses a +12 V DC adapter, which keeps mains wiring outside the box **[VERIFIED: both documents]**. The Etherwave manual adds that the instrument should not be used near water and needs ventilation **[VERIFIED]**.
- **Vintage tube instruments.** The RCA theremin uses a UX-280 rectifier tube and a mains power supply (RCATheremin.com). **Author's inference:** that means potentially lethal high voltages inside. Don't open or restore a vintage tube theremin without qualified help.
- **RF.** The patent chose its frequencies to avoid broadcast interference, and the Etherwave manual includes standard interference compliance text. **Author's inference:** a hobby build may need moving or shielding if it causes radio interference.

Prices are historical only. Statements about stability, enclosures, and RF behavior are labeled as my inferences. ### Recommended first build

1. **For a first theremin that actually plays well:** the **Open.Theremin V4**, or a commercial **Moog Etherwave** kit if one is available. Both come with documentation, calibration aids, and community support, and the Open.Theremin is fully open-source, which suits Juan's open, educational work. *(This is my recommendation, not a source's.)*
2. **For learning the physics from scratch:** **Moog's 1996 EM Theremin** article, with Art Harrison's simpler designs as stepping stones. Treat the PAiA Theremax manual as a second worked example of the same principles.
3. **For history only:** Moog's 1954 tube design. Read it; don't build it as a first project.

## What the record does not support (summary of corrections)

- Theremin's founding paper is from 1921–22, not 1925.
- The Stalin Prize was for Buran (1947), not for the Great Seal bug (1945).
- Toscanini and Rachmaninoff attended the private Plaza demonstration (24 January 1928), not necessarily the public Met concert.
- The 1938 departure is disputed: kidnapping vs voluntary vs intelligence and debt.
- He was in a Kolyma (Magadan) camp, but whether he worked in gold mines or on roads and construction is disputed.
- At MSU he was a mechanic, not a professor.
- The Conservatory dismissal most likely came in 1967, not 1971.
- Bourges was 1989; Stanford was 1991.
- "Good Vibrations" used the Electro-Theremin.
- Hand capacitance is under 1 pF, not "a few pF."
- Moog's 1954 article was in *Radio & Television News*.
