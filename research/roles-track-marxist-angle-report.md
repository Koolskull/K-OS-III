## Part A: The Marxist-feminist case, from primary sources

### A1. Friedrich Engels, *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884)

**Where to read it:** marxists.org, Chapter II "The Family". It is split into sections. The two you need are:
- §3 "The Pairing Family": https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1884/origin-family/ch02c.htm [V]
- §4 "The Monogamous Family": https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1884/origin-family/ch02d.htm [V]

**Correction to the brief.** The "world historical defeat" sentence is in **§3, "The Pairing Family"** (ch02c), not §4. Cite it that way.

**Key passages (all [V], quoted from marxists.org):**

1. Defeat of the female sex (Ch. II §3):
   > "The overthrow of mother-right was the world historical defeat of the female sex. The man took command in the home also; the woman was degraded and reduced to servitude, she became the slave of his lust and a mere instrument for the production of children."

2. Monogamy as a property institution (Ch. II §4):
   > "It was the first form of the family to be based, not on natural, but on economic conditions – on the victory of private property over primitive, natural communal property. The Greeks themselves put the matter quite frankly: the sole exclusive aims of monogamous marriage were to make the man supreme in the family, and to propagate, as the future heirs to his wealth, children indisputably his own."

3. The first class antagonism (§4):
   > "The first class opposition that appears in history coincides with the development of the antagonism between man and woman in monogamous marriage, and the first class oppression coincides with that of the female sex by the male. Monogamous marriage was a great historical step forward; nevertheless, together with slavery and private wealth, it opens the period that has lasted until today in which every step forward is also relatively a step backward..."

4. Housework becomes private service, and the double bind (§4):
   > "Household management lost its public character. It no longer concerned society. It became a private service; the wife became the head servant, excluded from all participation in social production... if she carries out her duties in the private service of her family, she remains excluded from public production and unable to earn; and if she wants to take part in public production and earn independently, she cannot carry out family duties."

5. The famous analogy (§4):
   > "Within the family he is the bourgeois and the wife represents the proletariat."

6. The program (§4):
   > "the first condition for the liberation of the wife is to bring the whole female sex back into public industry, and that this in turn demands the abolition of the monogamous family as the economic unit of society."

7. The often-missed twist (§4):
   > "Prostitution disappears; monogamy, instead of collapsing, at last becomes a reality – also for men."

   Followed by:
   > "Private housekeeping is transformed into a social industry. The care and education of the children becomes a public affair..."

**Steelman.** Engels's lasting contribution isn't his prehistory. It's a materialist question: *what economic function does a family form serve, and who controls the property it passes on?* He argues that the strict monogamous household grew up around inheritance of private wealth, and that this turned household management from socially recognized work into private, unpaid "service." His double-bind passage (paid work or family duties, not both) predicts, in 1884, the "second shift" that sociologists measured a century later. He also isn't anti-monogamy in the popular sense. He calls it "a great historical step forward" and predicts it will "at last become a reality – also for men" once it stops resting on property. For the Roles track, the useful core is this: what gets called "traditional" in family roles is tied to particular property and labor arrangements, and those change.

**Where Engels is weakest** (see B3): his anthropology came from Lewis Henry Morgan's unilineal stage theory, and modern anthropology has rejected it.

### A2. Alexandra Kollontai, "Communism and the Family" (1920)

**Source:** marxists.org, first published in *Komunistka* No. 2 (1920): https://www.marxists.org/archive/kollonta/1920/communism-family.htm [V]

**Key passages ([V]):**

> "The family breaks down as more and more women go out to work."

> "Just as housework withers away, so the obligations of parents to their children wither away gradually until finally society assumes the full responsibility."

> "Working mothers have no need to be alarmed; communist [society is] not intending to take children away from their parents or to tear the baby from the breast of its mother, and neither is it planning to take, violent measures to destroy the family."

> "There is no escaping the fact: the old type of family has had its day. The family is withering away not because it is being forcibly destroyed by the state, but because the family is ceasing to be a necessity. The state does not need the family, because the domestic economy is no longer profitable..."

> "...the narrow and exclusive affection of the mother for her own children must expand until it extends to all the children of the great, proletarian family..."

**Steelman.** Kollontai is honest about a real problem: the factory economy was already pulling working mothers out of the home and leaving them with "difficult and exhausting tasks" on top of factory hours. Her answer was to socialize those tasks (public kitchens, crèches, laundries) so working women got "the same ease and light, hygiene and beauty that previously only the very rich could afford." That is a serious welfare argument, and many non-Marxist states later adopted pieces of it, such as childcare and school meals.

**Why this text matters for the counter-case.** This is the clearest primary-source evidence that some Marxist theorists *did* expect, and welcome, the family "withering away," and said "the state does not need the family." A conservative lesson can quote it fairly. It is documented record, not inference. See B2 for what happened next (the 1936 reversal).

### A3. Mariarosa Dalla Costa & Selma James; Silvia Federici, *Wages Against Housework* (1975)

**Wages Against Housework.** Silvia Federici, Power of Women Collective / Falling Wall Press, 1975. PDF: https://www.monoskop.org/images/2/23/Federici_Silvia_Wages_Against_Housework_1975.pdf [V]. Key lines ([V]):

> "They say it is love. We say it is unwaged work."

> "...to want wages for housework means to refuse that work as the expression of our nature, and therefore to refuse precisely the female role that capital has invented for us."

> "We are housemaids, prostitutes, nurses, shrinks; this is the essence of the 'heroic' spouse who is celebrated on 'Mother's Day'."

**Dalla Costa & James, *The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community*** (Falling Wall Press; bibliographies give 1972 or 1973) [U, date varies by edition]. This is the founding text of the Wages for Housework current. It argues that unwaged domestic labor produces the commodity labor-power and so is part of capitalist production. I didn't retrieve the text, so treat this as a paraphrase.

**Steelman.** This school makes the strongest case that home labor is real, grueling, productive work. Lessons 4 and 6 want to say exactly that. The insight is that calling domestic care "love" or "nature" has an economic effect: it makes the work free. Federici's line "the female role that capital has invented" is the contested part. But "housework is work that the economy depends on and doesn't pay for" is close to the national-accounts debate about unpaid household production. It also shows up, surprisingly, in Catholic social teaching (see E: *Laborem Exercens* calls motherhood "toil" and proposes "grants to mothers").

### A4. Silvia Federici, *Caliban and the Witch: Women, the Body and Primitive Accumulation* (Autonomedia, 2004)

**Source:** The Anarchist Library edition, "mostly based on the first edition": https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/silvia-federici-caliban-and-the-witch [V]

**Chapters** ([V], from the table of contents):
1. "All the World Needs a Jolt" (medieval peasant and heretic struggles)
2. "The Accumulation of Labor and the Degradation of Women" (includes "Land Privatization in Europe, the Production of Scarcity, and the Separation of Production from Reproduction," "Women: The New Commons and the Substitute for the Lost Land," and "The Patriarchy of the Wage")
3. "The Great Caliban" (the body as a work-machine)
4. "The Great Witch-Hunt in Europe"
5. "Colonization and Christianization"

**Key passages ([V]):**

Introduction, her three additions to Marx's primitive accumulation:
> "(i) the development of a new sexual division of labor subjugating women's labor and women's reproductive function to the reproduction of the work-force; (ii) the construction of a new patriarchal order, based upon the exclusion of women from waged-work and their subordination to men; (iii) the mechanization of the proletarian body and its transformation, in the case of women, into a machine for the production of new workers."

Introduction:
> "...the body has been for women in capitalist society what the factory has been for male waged workers: the primary ground of their exploitation and resistance..."

Ch. 2, "The Patriarchy of the Wage":
> "...the family emerges in the period of primitive accumulation also as the most important institution for the appropriation and concealment of women's labor."

Ch. 4:
> "...the witch-hunt was one of the most important events in the development of capitalist society and the formation of the modern proletariat."

**Steelman.** Federici's strongest point is documented and widely accepted. Between the late Middle Ages and the Napoleonic codes, European women lost legal and economic standing: exclusion from guilds and trades, legal minority, and harsher policing of reproduction. Even her harshest critics agree with that (see B3: Kindo & Darmangeat write that the transition "was clearly accompanied, in Europe, by a general strengthening of male domination"). Her framing, that capitalism needed a cheap, controlled supply of new workers and that women's bodies and unpaid work were enclosed the way common land was, fits the track's "curated vignette" theme well. She argues that a "new model of femininity emerged: the ideal woman and wife—passive, obedient, thrifty, of few words, always busy at work, and chaste" by the end of the 17th century [V], meaning the housewife ideal was built over time, not natural.

**Biggest weakness (verified in her own text).**
- In Ch. 4 she says "hundreds of thousands of women were burned, hanged, and tortured in less than two centuries."
- Her own footnote [145] gives Anne Barstow's estimate of "at least 100,000" killed, plus regional counts (Midelfort: at least 3,200 in Southwestern Germany 1560–1670; Larner: 4,500 in Scotland 1590–1650).
- Mainstream estimates are about 40,000–60,000 executions (see B3).

### A5. Heidi Hartmann, "The Unhappy Marriage of Marxism and Feminism: Towards a More Progressive Union" (*Capital & Class* 3(2), 1979). Dual-systems theory

**Source:** PDF reprint, https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~hoganr/SOC%20602/Hartmann_1979.pdf [V]

**Key passages ([V]):**

> "The 'marriage' of marxism and feminism has been like the marriage of husband and wife depicted in English common law: marxism and feminism are one, and that one is marxism."

> "Marxist categories, like capital itself, are sex-blind. The categories of marxism cannot tell us who will fill the empty places."

> "...we define patriarchy as a set of social relations which has a material base and in which there are hierarchical relations between men and solidarity among them which enable them in turn to dominate women. The material base of patriarchy is men's control over women's labor power."

> "Instead of fighting for equal wages for men and women, male workers sought the 'family wage', wanting to retain their wives' services at home. In the absence of patriarchy a unified working class might have confronted capitalism, but patriarchal social relations divided the working class..."

**Steelman.** Hartmann criticizes Marxism from inside the left. Class analysis alone ("sex-blind") can't explain why women end up doing the unpaid work. Her historical claim matters most for Lesson 2 ("curated vignette"). The "male breadwinner / homemaker" family wasn't a timeless default. It was partly a **labor-movement bargain** of the late 19th and early 20th century: unions pushed for a wage big enough to support a dependent wife. This is checkable history, and it overlaps with Catholic teaching (see *Rerum Novarum* §45 and *Laborem Exercens* §19 in B2), which also championed the family wage, for different reasons.

### A6. Social Reproduction Theory: Lise Vogel (1983) and Tithi Bhattacharya, ed. (2017)

- Lise Vogel, *Marxism and the Oppression of Women: Toward a Unitary Theory* (Rutgers UP, 1983; reissued by Brill/Haymarket, 2013) [U, contents not retrieved].
- Tithi Bhattacharya (ed.), *Social Reproduction Theory: Remapping Class, Recentering Oppression* (Pluto Press, 2017) [U, contents not retrieved].

**Paraphrase (unverified, check before quoting).** Social reproduction theory (SRT) tries to replace dual-systems theory with a single framework. Capitalism depends on the daily and generational "reproduction" of workers: birth, feeding, care, schooling. That work happens mostly outside the market, mostly by women. In this view, women's oppression comes from that structural role, not from a separate "patriarchy" system.

**Steelman.** SRT gives a serious, non-conspiratorial vocabulary for Lesson 4: the work of making and keeping people alive is the base the paid economy stands on. Fraser's 2016 essay (A7) is the most widely read version of it.

**Key passages ([V]):**

Guardian 2013:
> "Second-wave feminism emerged as a critique of the first but has become the handmaiden of the second."

**Steelman, and why this matters most for the track.** **Fraser, a socialist-feminist philosopher at the New School, is making much the same complaint the owner makes.** Mainstream ("lean in") feminism helped push women into the paid labor force on employers' terms. Two-earner households became the norm, and care work got squeezed. She writes from the left, so her critique is very hard to wave away as reactionary. For Lesson 1 ("two polite positions"), she shows that criticism of corporate feminism isn't owned by the right. That gives the lesson an honest third (and fourth) position, and it means the track doesn't need any "Marxist conspiracy" claim to make its main point.

### A-bis.5 The "proxy tentacles" question: what the record actually shows

The owner's lesson framing suggests that Marxist "proxy tentacles" with "evil intentions" shaped modern gender roles. Here is what the documented record supports, and what it doesn't.

- **Documented:**
  - Some Marxist theorists explicitly predicted and welcomed the family "withering away" (Kollontai 1920, A2).
  - Engels called for "the abolition of the monogamous family as the economic unit of society" (A1).
  - The USSR sponsored and monitored international women's organizations. Yulia Gradskova, "The WIDF's Work for Women's Rights in the (Post)colonial Countries and the 'Soviet Agenda'," *International Review of Social History* (2022), https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-review-of-social-history/article/widfs-work-for-womens-rights-in-the-postcolonial-countries-and-the-soviet-agenda/175C334558518191679FE686E5367781 [V], shows CPSU Central Committee files tracking the WIDF. She also concludes that Soviet "intentions were quite far from reality," that delegates pursued their own agendas, and that Soviet material hid the "double burden" women carried at home.
  - Daniel Horowitz has documented Betty Friedan's earlier work in left-wing/Popular Front union journalism ("Rethinking Betty Friedan and *The Feminine Mystique*," *American Quarterly* 48(1), 1996) [V-sec].
- **Documented on the other side:**
  - Gloria Steinem's Independent Research Service in 1959–62 was **CIA-funded**, a fact she later acknowledged [V-sec].
  - The Cold War shaped U.S. feminism from both directions.
- **Not documented:**
  - Any single coordinating actor behind mainstream U.S. feminism.
  - Intent ("evil intentions") can't be shown from these sources.
  - Marxist feminists themselves (Fraser; Arruzza/Bhattacharya/Fraser) are among the sharpest critics of corporate "lean in" feminism. That counts against the idea of a unified Marxist project behind it.
- **Verdict for the lessons:** "influence" is documented. "Coordination" and "malice" are the owner's interpretation. EPISTEMIC_STANCE requires labeling it that way and avoiding "conspiracy-as-identity framing in either direction."

## Part B: Counter-steelmen

### B1. Liberal feminism (equal rights, individual choice)

**Best case.** The changes since 1960 came mostly from legal equality, contraception, education, and market demand for women's skills, not from Marxist theory. The education figures (58.5% of bachelor's degrees in 2021–22, A-bis.4) and women's longer lives fit a story of expanding individual choice. On this view, "roles" should be whatever free adults negotiate. Marxist critics (Fraser) mistake a choice many women value, paid work, for "capitalism's handmaiden." Liberal feminists can also say the Marxist record on women in power was poor: the Soviet "double burden" (Gradskova) and the 1936 abortion reversal (B2).

**Strongest point:** it explains the observed rise in women's education and earnings without any coordinating agent. **Weakest point:** it struggles with the Stevenson & Wolfers happiness paradox and with how unpaid care work is valued. Fraser and the conservatives both attack it there.

### B2. Conservative / Catholic social teaching

**Primary sources (vatican.va English texts):**
- Leo XIII, *Rerum Novarum* (15 May 1891), https://www.vatican.va/content/leo-xiii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_l-xiii_enc_15051891_rerum-novarum.html [V]:
  - §4 rejects the socialist remedy of abolishing private property.
  - §12 calls the family "a true society, and one older than any State."
  - §14: "The socialists, therefore, in setting aside the parent and setting up a State supervision, act against natural justice, and destroy the structure of the home."
  - §42: "Women, again, are not suited for certain occupations; a woman is by nature fitted for home-work, and it is that which is best adapted at once to preserve her modesty and to promote the good upbringing of children..."
  - §45: wages "ought not to be insufficient to support a frugal and well-behaved wage-earner."
- John Paul II, *Laborem Exercens* (14 Sept 1981), §19, https://www.vatican.va/content/john-paul-ii/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_jp-ii_enc_14091981_laborem-exercens.html [V]:
  - "...there must be a social re-evaluation of the mother's role, of the toil connected with it, and of the need that children have for care, love and affection..."
  - It supports a family wage "or through other social measures such as family allowances or grants to mothers devoting themselves exclusively to their families."
  - Women should be able to work "without being discriminated against and without being excluded from jobs for which they are capable."
- **The Soviet record.** The USSR legalized abortion by decree on 18 Nov 1920 and banned it again by decree on 27 June 1936, alongside measures meant to strengthen the family [V-sec]. Conservatives read this as Marxism's own admission that "withering away the family" (Kollontai) failed in practice.
- Contemporary voices in this vein include Mary Harrington, *Feminism Against Progress* (2023), and Louise Perry, *The Case Against the Sexual Revolution* (2022). They are sometimes called "reactionary feminists." Their texts weren't retrieved [U]; only their general positions are described here.

**Best case.** The family is older than the state and prior to it. Mothers' work is real "toil" that deserves social support, not just a place in the labor market. The Catholic tradition, notably, **rejects both** the Marxist plan to absorb the family into the state and the liberal-market plan to absorb women into wage labor. It is a genuine third position, not just the "conservative pole."

**Weakest point:** *Rerum Novarum* §42 ("by nature fitted for home-work") is a nature claim that the empirical literature (B3, D6) doesn't support beyond pregnancy and lactation. *Laborem Exercens* itself moved toward women's access to "jobs for which they are capable."

### B3. Empirical and historiographic critiques of the Marxist story

- **Engels's anthropology.** *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, "matriarchy," https://www.britannica.com/topic/matriarchy [V]. The entry describes the 19th-century unilineal evolutionary scheme (Bachofen, Morgan, Engels) as "now discredited." It also says societies ruled by women in the strong sense "have never existed" in the ethnographic record, though matrilineal societies do exist. Cynthia Eller, *The Myth of Matriarchal Prehistory* (2000), is a feminist critique along the same lines [U, not retrieved].
- **Federici's witch-hunt history.** Yann Kindo & Christophe Darmangeat, "Caliban and the Witch: A Critical Analysis" (2017; English translation on libcom), https://libcom.org/article/caliban-and-witch-critical-analysis-yann-kindo-and-christophe-darmangeat [V]. Both authors are themselves on the left. They argue:
  - Execution totals were about **40,000–60,000** (the mainstream estimate), not "hundreds of thousands."
  - Citing Alison Rowlands, men were the **majority** of those accused in places such as Iceland, Normandy, Estonia, and Russia.
  - Federici misuses an image of Christine de Pizan as an illustration.
  - The claim that midwives were especially targeted doesn't hold up.
  - But they concede that the early modern period "was clearly accompanied, in Europe, by a general strengthening of male domination."
- **"False consciousness."** Engels coined the phrase in a letter to Franz Mehring, 14 July 1893, https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1893/letters/93_07_14.htm [V]. He describes ideology as "a process accomplished by the so-called thinker consciously, indeed, but with a false consciousness. The real motives impelling him remain unknown to him." **Critics' point:** this claim can't be falsified. A woman who says she prefers homemaking can always be told her preference is "false." Liberals and conservatives both object, and so do some feminists. (The owner's "matrix" frame has the same structure. See Part E.)
- **Sex differences.** The meta-analytic record is mixed, and both sides overstate it:
  - Janet Shibley Hyde, "The Gender Similarities Hypothesis," *American Psychologist* 60(6), 2005 [V-sec, abstract]: about **78%** of the gender-difference effects she reviewed were small or near zero.
  - Del Giudice, Booth & Irwing, *PLoS ONE* 7(1), 2012 [V-sec, abstract]: the multivariate personality difference was **D = 2.71** when measured across 15 latent factors.
  - Daniel Voyer, Susan Voyer & Philip Bryden (1995) and later meta-analyses find larger male advantages in mental rotation [U, not retrieved; check the effect size before citing]. Smaller differences show up elsewhere: Thompson & Voyer (2014) on emotion recognition, a female advantage of roughly **d ≈ 0.19** [V-sec]. Sorokowski et al. on olfaction, female advantage **g ≈ 0.08–0.30** depending on the measure [V-sec].
  - Neither "no differences" (strong social constructionism) nor "different natures dictate roles" (*Rerum Novarum* §42) is supported as stated.

**Strongest counter overall.** Marxist feminism's *historical narratives* (Engels's prehistory, Federici's body counts) are its weakest parts and have been corrected by scholars on the left. Its *analytical questions* (who does unpaid work, and who benefits) are its strongest parts and are now shared by Catholic teaching and mainstream time-use economics.

## Part C: Honest assessment

1. **The strongest Marxist-feminist contribution is a question, not a verdict:** *whose labor makes the household run, who pays for it, and who benefits?* Engels (1884), Wages for Housework (1975), Hartmann (1979), and SRT all ask it. Measured data back it up (ATUS 2024 time gaps), and *Laborem Exercens* §19 says much the same thing in Catholic terms. A Roles track can teach this question without endorsing Marxism.
2. **The strongest overlap with the owner's view comes from the left.** Fraser's argument that second-wave feminism became "capitalism's handmaiden" and pushed families into two earners and a "triple or quadruple shift" is close to the owner's complaint. That makes "Marxists did this to the family" too simple. The most-cited Marxist feminists are *against* the corporate feminism the owner dislikes.
3. **The weakest parts are its histories:**
   - Engels's matriarchal prehistory is discredited (Britannica).
   - Federici's "hundreds of thousands" is contradicted by her own cited sources and by Kindo & Darmangeat.
   - "False consciousness" can't be falsified.
4. **The Soviet record is the strongest evidence against its programs.** Kollontai's "the state does not need the family" was followed by the 1936 reversal and a hidden "double burden" (Gradskova).
5. **The conspiracy claim isn't established.** Soviet influence efforts (WIDF) are documented. So is CIA funding on the U.S. side (Steinem's IRS). A coordinated Marxist plan with "evil intentions" behind modern U.S. gender roles is **not** documented in anything I retrieved. Per EPISTEMIC_STANCE, it should be labeled the owner's interpretive frame, not taught as fact.
6. **The data really are mixed.** Each school picks its own numbers:
   - Women's education (58.5% of BAs) and life expectancy (81.1) favor liberal and Marxist readings of progress.
   - Record-low fertility (TFR 1,599.5), late marriage (28.4/30.8), and the female happiness paradox favor conservative readings of loss.
   - None of these numbers establishes causation. Lessons should say so.

2. **Curated vignette.**
   - Teach the 1950s breadwinner family as a specific, partly bargained arrangement, using Coontz ("historical fluke"; 25% poor; more than 40% of Black mothers of small children working) and Hartmann (the union "family wage").

   - Use Martha Ballard (816 births) to show women's skilled work before industrialization.
   - Address the relativism objection directly: "roles changed" doesn't mean "anything goes." Conservatives argue some roles change while others (gestation, nursing) don't.
4. **Grueling labor.**
   - Use Sumner 1910 (73½-hour weeks at Lowell; 15–18-hour days for needlewomen; 58.1% of employed women in domestic service in 1870), ATUS 2024 (2.34 vs. 1.67 hrs/day), Wages for Housework ("They say it is love. We say it is unwaged work."), and Proverbs 31:13–27 (KJV).

5. **Fear-manifestation loop.**
   - There is no Marxist-feminist content to bring in here. **Label the lesson as devotional/psychological.**

   - Add a caveat against victim-blaming: fear doesn't cause structural harms like poverty or abuse.
6. **Flow and the two gifts.**
   - "Things men cannot do" is literally true only for **gestation and lactation**. Say that precisely.
   - Average perceptual and personality differences exist but are mostly small (Hyde 2005: about 78% small or near zero). Multivariate measures are larger (Del Giudice 2012: D = 2.71). Present this as a live scientific debate.
   - Label "prison-planet matrix" and "Father's flow" as the **owner's metaphysical frame**, consistent with the two-door model in VISION.md.

## Part E: Connection to the owner's project

- **Prison-planet matrix vs. "false consciousness."** These are structurally the same. Each says people misperceive their real situation because of a system that hides itself (Engels to Mehring, 1893). The metaphysics differ: material class interest vs. a spiritual or cosmic order. The track should say this plainly. It's a good critical-thinking lesson, and it applies the same unfalsifiability critique to both (B3).
- **Father's flow.** This is devotional content, and it belongs behind the faith "door" (VISION.md: "Two audiences. Two doors. One house."). It shouldn't be presented as an empirical claim about gender.
- **Two-door identity.** Keep the neutral door neutral. Marxist feminism, Catholic teaching, and liberal feminism are presented as named schools. The owner's frame is presented as the owner's.
- **Rerum Novarum as a third position.** This is the most useful structural move available. Catholic social teaching rejects both socialism (*RN* §4, §14) and labor markets that treat workers' families as costs (*RN* §45). *Laborem Exercens* §19 agrees with Wages for Housework that motherhood is "toil" that deserves material support ("grants to mothers"). Fraser's call for "a form of life that de-centres waged work and valorises unwaged activities, including... carework" lands in nearly the same place from the left. Showing that convergence is more honest, and more interesting, than a left-vs-right frame. It also fits K-OS's sovereignty and self-reliance themes without pointing at a villain.
