# MKUltra, Stargate, and declassified intelligence programs

## Short version

Two clusters of U.S. intelligence activity are now partially declassified and well documented:

**MKUltra** (and related earlier efforts under names such as Bluebird and Artichoke) was a CIA program, roughly 1953–1973, that tested drugs (especially LSD), hypnosis, sensory deprivation, and other techniques for interrogation and behavior influence. Many subjects were unwitting. Large portions of the records were destroyed in 1973 on orders associated with then-DCI Richard Helms. The Church Committee (1975–76) and subsequent releases established the basic facts of illegal domestic experimentation and ethical violations.

**Stargate** (and predecessor programs such as Grill Flame, Center Lane, and Sun Streak) was a series of Defense Intelligence / Army / CIA efforts, primarily 1970s–1995, that investigated remote viewing for intelligence collection. An external evaluation by the American Institutes for Research in September 1995 concluded that while laboratory statistics showed anomalies, the product was too vague for operational use and had not guided intelligence operations. The program was terminated. Substantial files have been declassified.

These are real, documented cases of overreach and secrecy. They demonstrate that intelligence agencies have conducted unethical human experimentation and have pursued fringe methods when they believed national security required it. They do not, by themselves, prove a single continuous cabal directing all later events.

## Expanded notes

### MKUltra

Allen Dulles authorized the expanded program in 1953. Sidney Gottlieb, a CIA chemist in the Technical Services orbit, was central to the drug and behavioral work. Experiments occurred in universities, hospitals, prisons, and safehouses. Front organizations funded research without always informing the investigators of the ultimate sponsor. The 1963 CIA Inspector General review of MKUltra was an internal critique that contributed to scaling back unwitting testing; the 1973 destruction order then severely limited later investigation. Surviving financial records, residual files, testimony, and later FOIA releases still confirm the core activities.

**Primary places to read:**

- Church Committee materials — U.S. Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities (1975–76). The foundational public congressional record on domestic intelligence abuses of that era, including behavioral research.
- National Security Archive collection *CIA and the Behavioral Sciences: Mind Control, Drug Experiments and MKULTRA* — curated set of more than 1,200 records (predecessors Bluebird/Artichoke, MKUltra subprojects, related investigations). Public briefing material and document highlights are available via nsarchive.gwu.edu; the full scholarly set is distributed through the Digital National Security Archive.
- Surviving declassified CIA files in the [CIA Reading Room](https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/) (search MKUltra, Bluebird, Artichoke, Gottlieb).
- The 1963 Report of Inspection of MKUltra (CIA Inspector General) — a key internal document that survived and informed later oversight.

Mainstream historical accounts treat MKUltra as a Cold War excess driven by fear of Soviet and Chinese brainwashing techniques, combined with institutional secrecy and weak internal constraints. Expanded or dissenting accounts sometimes link it to longer traditions of behavioral control or to later, less documented programs. The verified core is the Church Committee record, the IG materials, and the surviving/FOIA files; broader claims require separate evidence.

Secondary synthesis that rests on the declassified record includes John Marks, *The Search for the "Manchurian Candidate"* (1979), and later treatments such as Stephen Kinzer, *Poisoner in Chief*.

### Stargate / remote viewing

The programs arose in part from concern that the Soviet Union was investing in psychic research. Remote viewers were tasked against various intelligence targets. Results were mixed: some individual sessions were later claimed as hits by participants; the formal external evaluation found the overall product insufficient for operational use.

**The 1995 AIR evaluation** — *An Evaluation of Remote Viewing: Research and Applications* (Michael D. Mumford, Andrew M. Rose, David A. Goslin; American Institutes for Research; 29 September 1995), commissioned as CIA prepared to decide the program’s future — is the capstone official review. It included separate assessments by statistician Jessica Utts (who argued the laboratory effect was real) and psychologist Ray Hyman (who disputed that the statistics established paranormal functioning). The joint operational conclusion was clear: information was vague and ambiguous; in no case had remote viewing guided an actual intelligence operation; continued use for intelligence gathering was not warranted. The program was terminated; records were declassified over following years.

**Primary places to read:**

- CIA Reading Room Stargate / remote-viewing collections.
- American Institutes for Research, *An Evaluation of Remote Viewing: Research and Applications* (29 September 1995). Widely mirrored; searchable under that title.
- Participant memoirs (e.g. Joseph McMoneagle) as first-person accounts — to be weighed against the formal evaluation, not substituted for it.

### How to read this material

Treat the declassified record as established fact where the documents and congressional record agree. Treat interpretations that connect every subsequent intelligence or cultural phenomenon to these programs as hypotheses that still need evidence. The existence of real abuse does not automatically validate every later claim of continuous control. The reverse is also true: the termination of a named program does not prove that related research or techniques simply vanished.

## Sources

**MKUltra / behavioral research**

- Church Committee reports and related Senate materials (U.S. Senate Select Committee to Study Governmental Operations with Respect to Intelligence Activities, 1975–76).
- National Security Archive, *CIA and the Behavioral Sciences: Mind Control, Drug Experiments and MKULTRA* (collection highlights and briefing books at nsarchive.gwu.edu).
- CIA Reading Room — search MKUltra, Bluebird, Artichoke, and related cryptonyms.
- CIA Inspector General, Report of Inspection of MKUltra (26 July 1963).
- John Marks, *The Search for the "Manchurian Candidate"* (1979) — secondary synthesis built on FOIA primary documents.
- Stephen Kinzer, *Poisoner in Chief* — later secondary treatment of Gottlieb and the program, grounded in the declassified record.

**Stargate / remote viewing**

- American Institutes for Research, *An Evaluation of Remote Viewing: Research and Applications* (Mumford, Rose, Goslin; 29 September 1995).
- CIA Reading Room Stargate and predecessor-program releases (Grill Flame, Center Lane, Sun Streak, etc.).
- Participant accounts (e.g. McMoneagle) as primary experiential claims, not as the official evaluation.

**How to use the sources**

Start with the Church Committee summary material and the 1995 AIR executive conclusions. Then open specific declassified files from the CIA Reading Room or National Security Archive highlights. Secondary books come after the primary record, not before.
