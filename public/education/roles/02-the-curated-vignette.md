## What this lesson is for

To name precisely what the “traditional family” image usually shows — a particular American mid-20th-century household — and to contrast it with the record of women’s labor across earlier eras and cultures.

> 🗣️ “The conservative position on what women are expected to do is also very warped and referential of a curated portrayal of a particular vignette of history rather than an actual portrayal of historically traditional family roles.”

This claim aligns with a mainstream historical correction: the 1950s image is not the whole past.

---

## What the record shows about women’s labor

- Household production and market work interwove. Alice Clark (Working Life of Women in the Seventeenth Century, 1919) and Laurel Thatcher Ulrich (Good Wives, 1982; A Midwife’s Tale, 1990) document early modern and colonial women as producers: agriculture, textiles, midwifery, shopkeeping, credit.
- Industrial-era “labor-saving” devices often shifted work to women rather than eliminating it (Ruth Schwartz Cowan, More Work for Mother, 1983).
- Wage work expanded but did not erase care burdens; sectoral segregation and “greedy jobs” limited equality of outcomes (Claudia Goldin, Understanding the Gender Gap, 1990; Career and Family, 2021).
- Cross-cultural snapshot: Proverbs 31 (primary source) describes a wife who manages fields, trade, and household — a composite of economic and domestic labor, not a narrow domesticity.

Mainstream label: the curated vignette is incomplete. Dissenting label: some contemporary writers argue that a return to complementary roles yields better outcomes (e.g., Schlafly 1977; more recently Perry 2022 and Harrington 2023). This track reads those arguments alongside the mainstream corrections, not as a settled reversal.
